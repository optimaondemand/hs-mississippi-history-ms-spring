📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1873 Mississippi voters chose a Union general from Maine as their governor. Two years later, the government he led was falling apart. What happened in between shows whether a new kind of politics could survive in a state that was not ready to accept it.
Reconstruction in Mississippi did not end in a single day. It wore down through night riding, hard bargaining, and a federal government that grew tired of the fight. This lesson covers the first half of that story: the Ku Klux Klan, the Republican government, and the governor who led it, Adelbert Ames.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
carpetbagger (KAR-pit-bag-er): An insulting name that Southern Democrats used for Northerners who moved South after the war and took part in Republican politics. The name suggested someone who arrived with only a cheap travel bag, hoping to profit.
Enforcement Acts (en-FORS-ment akts): Laws passed by Congress in 1870 and 1871 that made it a federal crime to use force or threats to keep citizens from voting, and that gave the president tools to act against groups like the Klan.
intimidation (in-tim-ih-DAY-shun): The use of fear or threats to force people to act, or not to act, in a certain way.
scalawag (SKAL-uh-wag): An insulting name for white Southerners who supported the Republican Party during Reconstruction.
📚 PART 1 · THE KLAN

Violence After Dark

After the Constitution of 1868, Black men in Mississippi voted for the first time, and they voted in large numbers. Black Mississippians made up a little more than half of the state's people, so any party that won most of their votes could win most elections. The Republican Party won them. Many white Democrats saw this as a danger to the only order they had ever known, and some of them decided to fight it with force.

The Ku Klux Klan began in Tennessee in 1866 and reached Mississippi by 1868. Its members rode at night, often in disguise. They whipped, burned out, and killed Black voters, Black teachers, and white Republicans. Their purpose was political. A man who is afraid to go to the polls does not vote, and a teacher who is driven out of a county leaves an empty school behind. This use of fear to control what people do is called intimidation.

In March 1871, violence broke out around a courthouse in Meridian. In the days that followed, a mob killed a number of Black men and drove Black Republican leaders out of town. Events like this showed that Mississippi's new voters had rights on paper but not yet the power to protect them.

📚 PART 2 · WASHINGTON ACTS

Washington Steps In, for a While

Congress answered the Klan with the Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871. These laws made it a federal crime to use force or threats to stop a citizen from voting. They also let the president use the army against groups that held whole counties in fear. Federal courts in Mississippi charged hundreds of men.

Most of those men received light punishments or none at all. Still, the effort made an impression. By the early 1870s the Klan was far less active in Mississippi. For a short time, the state government held the one thing every government must have: the power to protect its own citizens. The question was how long Washington would keep up the pressure. Marshals and soldiers cost money, and Northern voters were growing tired of the South.

📚 PART 3 · THE AMES GOVERNMENT

A Governor from Maine

Adelbert Ames was born in Maine in 1835 and trained at West Point. He won the Medal of Honor at the First Battle of Bull Run and rose to the rank of general. After the war he came to Mississippi as an army officer, served as the army's appointed governor in 1868, and then served as one of the state's two United States senators. In 1873 Mississippi voters elected him governor. His critics called him a carpetbagger, meaning a Northerner who came South to gain power. Ames saw himself as an officer who had stayed to finish a job.

🕰 THE ROAD TO 1875
1868
The Klan reaches Mississippi. Ames is placed in charge of the state by the army.
1870
Mississippi returns to the Union. Congress passes the first Enforcement Act. Ames becomes a senator.
1871
Violence at Meridian. A second Enforcement Act targets the Klan.
1873
Ames is elected governor.
1874
White taxpayers organize against the state's tax policy.

He led a government that looked very different from any in the state's past. Black Mississippians held high offices, including Alexander K. Davis as lieutenant governor, James Hill as secretary of state, and Thomas Cardozo as superintendent of education. The state paid for public schools for both white and Black children, something Mississippi had never tried at that scale. But schools, roads, and rebuilding cost money, and taxes rose. Most of the property owners who paid the bills were white, and many were angry. White taxpayers organized across the state to demand cuts and to attack the government as wasteful and corrupt. Some of the charges had substance, and others were politics.

Ames also needed allies among white Mississippians, and here his government struggled. James Alcorn, a wealthy planter and former slaveholder, had won the governor's office as a Republican in 1869 and had tried to build a party that included white landowners. Alcorn and Ames became rivals, and Ames defeated him in 1873. After that, many moderate whites who might have supported a Republican government turned against it. Whether a wider coalition could ever have lasted is one of the great what-ifs of this era. Alcorn himself was a scalawag, a white Southerner who joined the Republicans, and Democrats treated men like him as traitors.

📚 PART 4 · JUDGING THE GOVERNMENT

The Trouble with Winning

Classical thinkers asked a hard question of any government: can it keep the peace and apply its laws fairly to everyone? A government may win elections and still fail this test. By 1874, Ames's government could win the votes of Black citizens, but it could not win the obedience of a large share of white citizens, and it could not always protect its own voters.

A law that most people follow because they think it is fair is strong. A law that people follow only when soldiers stand nearby is weak. Ames governed a state where many white men refused to accept Black voting and where the Klan answered it with murder. Notice that these men were not defending the law. They were breaking it, and they broke it to change who held power.

At the same time, a fair reading must ask what Ames could have done differently. Could he have held on to more white moderates? Could he have cut taxes sooner? Historians disagree. What they agree on is that the outcome was not fixed in 1873. Mississippi's story over the next two years depended on choices made by governors, party leaders, and presidents.

ONE IDEA TO REMEMBER
Winning an election is not the same as ruling. A government must also keep the peace and make its laws stick.
