📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
On a Saturday in September 1875, Republicans gathered near Clinton, Mississippi, for a political rally and barbecue. By the end of the week, dozens of Black Mississippians were dead, and the result of the election to come was no longer in doubt.
Between 1874 and 1876, Mississippi Democrats carried out a campaign so effective that it earned its own name, the Mississippi Plan. This lesson explains what the Plan was, how it worked, and why Washington let it succeed. It ends with the question at the center of this week's discussion: was Reconstruction overthrown, or was it abandoned?

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
Enforcement Acts (en-FORS-ment akts) : Laws passed by Congress in 1870 and 1871 that made it a federal crime to use force or threats to keep citizens from voting, and that gave the president tools to act against groups like the Klan.
impeach (im-PEECH) : To formally charge a public official with wrongdoing. In Mississippi, the House brings the charges and the Senate decides whether the official is guilty.
Redeemer (ri-DEE-mer) : A white Southern Democrat who worked to take control of state government back from the Republicans. The name came from the claim that they would "redeem" the South.
rifle club (RY-ful klub) : An armed group of white Democrats that drilled in public and used force to influence the elections of 1875.
📚 PART 1 · THE WHITE LINE

Democrats Draw a Line

By 1874, many Democrats in Mississippi had decided that polite opposition would never remove the Republicans. Many called themselves Redeemers, meaning men who would redeem the state by taking it back. They organized what they called a white-line campaign: white men were to unite behind the Democratic Party, and white men who refused to join could expect to be shamed or threatened.

Democratic leaders described their goal as home rule, the right of Mississippians to govern themselves without Northern interference. To many white Mississippians that phrase sounded reasonable. In practice it meant a government that Black voters could not influence.

A turning point came in Vicksburg in December 1874. White citizens forced the Black sheriff of Warren County, Peter Crosby, out of office. Crosby appealed to Governor Ames. When armed Black men marched toward the city to support him, white forces met them, and dozens of Black men were killed. President Grant sent in federal troops to restore order. But Democrats drew a lesson from the episode: force worked, and Washington acted slowly.

The Plan had a clear logic. Democrats formed rifle clubs in nearly every county. These armed groups drilled in public, broke up Republican meetings, and made sure everyone knew what could happen to those who voted the wrong way. Historians also describe a strategy of picking fights at Republican gatherings and then pointing to the violence as proof that the Republicans were the troublemakers.

📚 PART 2 · CLINTON, 1875

A Week of Terror

On September 4, 1875, Republicans held a rally in Clinton, near Jackson. A large crowd of Black families came, and armed white Democrats came as well. A quarrel started, shots were fired, and the day turned into a massacre. In the days that followed, white men from nearby towns rode into the countryside and hunted down Black Mississippians. Many Black families hid in the woods, and many Republican leaders fled. Estimates of the dead run to several dozen, and no one can say the exact number.

Black Mississippians did not simply accept this. Many kept organizing, and some voted at great risk. Some Black men served in the militia companies, and others armed themselves to defend their homes. The courage of a voter who walked past armed men to reach a polling place is part of this story, even though those voters lost.

Ames telegraphed Washington to ask for troops. The reply from President Grant's government said that the public was tired of what it called yearly outbreaks of trouble in the South and that Mississippi should handle its own affairs. Left without help, and hoping to avoid a larger war, Ames made a deal with Democratic leaders. He would send his militia home, and they would keep the peace through the election. They did not keep their side of the bargain.

📚 PART 3 · THE ELECTION

The Plan Works

On Election Day in November 1875, armed men stood near polling places across the state. In some counties Black voters stayed home. In others they were driven away. In Yazoo County, where Black citizens far outnumbered whites, the Republican ticket received only a handful of votes. Democrats won the legislature by a huge margin. Democratic leaders claimed that the election was fair. Republican officials and Northern observers said it was won by terror, and a Senate committee would later side with them.

Once in office, the new legislature went after the Republican officials who were left. It removed Lieutenant Governor Davis. The House voted to impeach Governor Ames. Rather than face a trial, Ames resigned in March 1876 and left Mississippi. He lived until 1933, long enough to see how the new order that followed him in Mississippi turned out. The Redeemers now ran the state.

📚 PART 4 · OVERTHROWN OR ABANDONED?

Two Words, One Story

Some historians say that Reconstruction in Mississippi was overthrown. They point to the rifle clubs, the Clinton killings, and the terror on Election Day. Others say it was abandoned. They point to a federal government that had the power to protect Mississippi's voters and chose not to use it. The truth probably needs both words.

⚖️ THE TWO ARGUMENTS
Overthrown
Rifle clubs organized in nearly every county.
Clinton: a Republican rally became a massacre.
Black voters were driven from the polls in 1875.
Abandoned
Grant's government refused Ames's request for troops.
The Enforcement Acts were not used against the Plan.
Congress listened to the evidence in 1876 and did nothing.

In 1876 the Senate did look into what had happened. A committee led by Senator George Boutwell of Massachusetts collected testimony about the 1875 election in Mississippi. Senator Blanche K. Bruce, a Black Mississippian born into slavery, spoke on the Senate floor on March 31, 1876, asking Congress to protect the voters of his state. Congress did not act. In 1877 a disputed presidential election ended with Republican Rutherford B. Hayes in the White House and the last federal troops leaving Southern state capitals.

Notice how this story looks through a classical lens. The Redeemers did deliver something they promised: order. But they won it by using armed force against fellow citizens, and the peace they made rested on one side's defeat. A community held together by a shared good is stronger than one held together by fear. Mississippi would feel that difference for generations.
