📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1900, almost everything Mississippi earned came out of the ground, and most of it came from one white crop. Today the state's large employers build ships, assemble cars, and process chicken, and one of its famous farm products swims. How did an economy built on cotton change, and who decided that it would?
This lesson traces Mississippi's economy from the age of cotton to the age of factories and fish ponds. It asks what caused each change, who chose the next step, and whether those choices helped ordinary Mississippians prosper.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
aquaculture (AK-wuh-kul-chur): The raising of fish or other water animals, such as catfish, in ponds or tanks for sale as food.
boll weevil (bol WEE-vul): A small beetle that feeds on cotton plants and can destroy much of a crop.
bond (bond): A loan in the form of a certificate. A town or state sells bonds to raise money and promises to pay buyers back with interest.
incentive (in-SEN-tiv): A reward or benefit, such as a tax break, offered to persuade someone to make a particular choice.
📚 PART 1 · KING COTTON, WEAKENED

A State Built on One Crop

For about a century, cotton was the Mississippi economy. The rich soil of the Delta, the river, and, until 1865, enslaved labor made cotton the state's main source of money. After the Civil War, sharecropping, which you studied in Module 25, kept cotton at the center of life. Planters, merchants, and bankers all depended on the price of a single crop. When the price rose, the state boomed. When it fell, farm families fell into debt. An economy tied to one crop is fragile because a single bad event can hurt almost everyone at once.

Several bad events came. The boll weevil spread into Mississippi in the early 1900s and in some years ruined much of the crop. The flood of 1927 drowned fields across the Delta. Then, in the 1930s, the Great Depression pushed the price of cotton very low. You saw in Module 27 how these troubles piled up. Landowners and tenants both suffered, and many families, especially Black families, left Mississippi for jobs in Chicago, Detroit, and other northern cities.

Machines changed the farm next. A mechanical cotton picker, which could do the work of many hands, was shown to Delta planters at a demonstration near Clarksdale in 1944, and that day is often marked as a turning point. In the following decades, machines and new farm chemicals cut the labor a cotton farm needed. No law ordered this change. It came from thousands of separate decisions by landowners who wanted to lower their costs, and each decision could mean a family without work. Great events are sometimes made of many small choices.

📚 PART 2 · BRINGING IN FACTORIES

Balance Agriculture With Industry

By the 1930s many leaders believed that farming alone could not support the state. In 1936, during the Depression, the legislature passed a plan backed by Governor Hugh White. It was called Balance Agriculture With Industry, and people shortened it to BAWI. Under the plan, a town could sell a bond to raise money, build a factory, and rent it cheaply to a company that agreed to open a plant there. The company might also be excused from some local taxes for a number of years. Such offers are called incentives.

Who gained? Workers gained paychecks in places where cash was scarce. Towns gained a reason to hope. But the early plants mostly made clothing, furniture, and wood products, and the wages were low. The towns also took on a risk, because they had to pay back the bonds even if a company left. Supporters said the plan gave people jobs when nothing else did. Critics said it tied the state to low pay and to firms that could leave at any time. Both statements held some truth.

Ships came to the Gulf Coast as well. Ingalls Shipbuilding opened in Pascagoula in 1938. In the Second World War, which you studied in Module 27, it grew quickly, and it has long been among the state's larger employers. Its story shows that a few steady, skilled industries can matter more than many small ones.

📚 PART 3 · CATFISH AND CARS

New Crops, New Factories

Some change came from the farm itself. In the 1960s, farmers in the Delta began raising catfish in ponds dug into fields that had once grown cotton. This is called aquaculture. The Delta's flat land, plentiful water, and heavy clay soil, which holds water well, suited the work. Humphreys County and its seat, Belzoni, took the name Catfish Capital of the World, and Mississippi has long led the nation in farm-raised catfish. Processing plants now employ many workers. Foreign fish sold at lower prices have tested the industry in recent years, a reminder that no crop is safe forever.

Cars arrived later. Nissan opened a large plant at Canton in 2003, and Toyota opened one at Blue Springs in 2011. Both received large incentive packages from the state, the same idea as BAWI in a modern form. Each plant also drew smaller companies that make parts nearby. A plant like this offers better pay than the old sewing factory, but it also depends on decisions made in Japan and elsewhere.

💡 MISSISSIPPI TODAY
Casinos and Other Bets
In 1990 the legislature allowed casinos on the Mississippi River and the Gulf Coast, and the first opened in Biloxi in 1992. Casinos have brought jobs and tax money to coastal and river counties, and the Choctaw tribe operates its own resort near Philadelphia. People still disagree about whether such businesses lift a community or merely move money around. The details differ by county, and few economists claim that any one industry can carry a whole state.
📚 PART 4 · WHO DECIDED

Choices, Not Fate

Look back over the century. The boll weevil and the flood were accidents of nature. The mechanical picker was an invention, and the choice to use it belonged to landowners. BAWI, the catfish ponds, and the car plants were choices made by governors, legislators, farmers, and company leaders. Different choices were possible at each step. Had the legislature spent the BAWI bond money on schools, the state might look different today.

A classical thinker would ask two questions of any economy. First, does it produce enough for the people as a whole to prosper? Second, does it help citizens form good habits, such as saving, working steadily, and trusting their neighbors enough to do business? Mississippi's new industries answered the first question better than cotton did. Whether they answered the second is harder to say. In the next lesson you will ask why, even after so many changes, the state has stayed near the bottom in income.
