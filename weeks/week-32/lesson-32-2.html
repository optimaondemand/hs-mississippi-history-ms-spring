📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Mississippi has 82 counties. In each one, the courthouse is more than a building. It is where people record land deeds, serve on juries, pay property taxes, and, as you have seen, where in the 1960s they tried to register to vote. How does local government work, and what does a citizen owe it?
Most people meet their government at the county courthouse, the city hall, or the school board. This lesson shows how those local governments are built, who runs them, and what citizens are asked to do. It also asks why local self-government matters to a free people.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
beat (beet): In Mississippi, the name for each of the five districts of a county that elects a member of the board of supervisors.
board of supervisors (bord uv soo-per-VY-zerz): The five-member elected body that governs each Mississippi county, sets its tax rate, and oversees its roads and budget.
county seat (KOWN-tee seet): The town in a county where the courthouse and the main county offices are located.
mill (mil): A unit of property tax. One mill is one dollar of tax for every thousand dollars of a property's assessed value.
separation of powers (sep-uh-RAY-shun uv POW-erz): The idea that government power should be divided among separate branches so that no one group holds all of it.
📚 PART 1 · EIGHTY-TWO COUNTIES

The County and Its Seat

Mississippi is divided into 82 counties. A county is a part of the state that carries out state laws and provides local services such as roads, courts, and records. Each county has a county seat, the town where the courthouse and the main county offices are located. Some larger counties have two courthouse towns, such as Hinds County, which has Jackson and Raymond.

Counties came first in Mississippi history, long before most of its cities. Many of them are named for people you have read about, and many of the towns you have studied are county seats. Neshoba County's seat is Philadelphia. Sunflower County's is Indianola. Warren County's is Vicksburg. Lafayette County's is Oxford. Each courthouse reminds us where citizens go to take part in the life of their county.

What do counties do for the people who live in them? The courthouse is where a family records a deed to a piece of land, where a couple gets a marriage license, and where a person pays the tax on a car. It is also where trials are held and where jurors are called. Most people never visit the state capital, but nearly everyone has business at the county courthouse at some point.

💡 MISSISSIPPI HISTORY
Why So Many Small Counties?
It is often said that Mississippi's counties were drawn small so that a farmer could ride a horse to the county seat and back in one day. Whether or not that explanation is exact, the idea fits the way the state was settled. In the nineteenth century, travel was slow, roads were poor, and a trip to court or to record a deed was a major event. A county courthouse was the place where a man could register land, pay taxes, serve on a jury, and see the law at work. Many county courthouses are still among the grandest buildings in their towns, and many sit in the center of a square. The old pattern still shapes how Mississippians think about local government. People often feel closer to their county than to the state capital.
📚 PART 2 · THE BOARD OF SUPERVISORS

Five Beats, Five Supervisors

Each county is governed by a board of supervisors of five members. Each supervisor is elected from one of five districts, which Mississippians call beats. The board sets the county's tax rate, builds and repairs county roads and bridges, and approves the county budget. It usually meets at least once a month, and its meetings are open to the public.

Counties also elect other officers. The sheriff keeps order and runs the jail. The chancery clerk keeps land records and serves as clerk of the board. The circuit clerk keeps court records and, importantly, is in charge of voter registration. The tax assessor and collector sets the value of property and collects taxes. Each of these officers answers to the voters, not to the board. In this way the county, like the state, follows the separation of powers, in that no one person controls all of the county's work.

The way the five beats are drawn matters a great deal. In 1966, the legislature changed the rules to allow some counties to elect supervisors at large, so that all voters in a county could vote for all five seats. In counties where white voters were a majority, that would have made it hard for Black candidates to win. The Supreme Court ruled in 1969 that such changes needed federal review under the Voting Rights Act. A small rule about district lines can decide who holds power.

📚 PART 3 · MONEY AND SCHOOLS

How Local Governments Pay

Most county money comes from property taxes. The tax is measured in mills. One mill is one dollar of tax for every thousand dollars of assessed value. A home is assessed at only part of its market value. If a home is assessed at $10,000 and the rate is 100 mills, the owner pays $1,000 in tax. The board of supervisors sets the county rate, and schools and cities have rates of their own.

This matters because the people who set the rate and spend the money are neighbors. A supervisor who raises taxes may meet a voter at the grocery store the next day. That closeness has a good side, because officials can be held to account, and a bad side, because friends and family sometimes take care of one another first. Local government works best when citizens watch it.

Cities and towns have their own governments. Most have a mayor and a board of aldermen or a city council, elected by the voters. School districts have school boards, whose members are elected or appointed, depending on the district. The board hires a superintendent, sets the school budget, and decides how the district will use its money. These boards were at the center of the fight over desegregation, which is why who serves on them has long been important.

A school board is often the first office that a young adult can run for, and it is one of the few that lets a parent shape what happens in a classroom. Whoever sits on these boards helps decide what a community values and what it will pay for.

📚 PART 4 · THE CITIZEN

What a Citizen Owes

A free people must govern itself in small things before it can do so in large ones. Alexis de Tocqueville, who toured the United States in the 1830s, thought that local government was a school for citizens. In a town meeting or a county board, people learn to work with neighbors, argue without hatred, and deal with the real costs of public decisions.

What does a citizen owe? Voting is one duty. Serving on a jury is another. In many counties, jury lists are drawn from the voter rolls. That meant that keeping Black citizens off the rolls also kept them off juries, which helps explain why so many trials in this module ended as they did. Paying taxes, obeying laws, and attending public meetings are duties too.

Mississippi law also requires that most meetings of public boards be open to the public. Anyone may attend and listen. That law exists because a free people must be able to see how its money is spent and its decisions are made. A meeting no one attends is a meeting where only insiders have a say.

A citizen also has rights, and those rights are worth little unless they are used. People died for the right to register at a county courthouse. The duty to use it is part of what they left behind. In the discussion this week, you will judge whether the constitution that creates these offices is a good one. As you do, think about the citizens who must make it work.

💡 MISSISSIPPI TODAY
Your County Today
Most Mississippi counties post their meeting schedules and minutes online, and the board meetings are open to the public. If you want to see local government at work, you can look up your own county's board of supervisors, find out who represents your beat, and read what the board has decided. You may find that a few votes there affect your road, your school, or your property more directly than anything decided in Jackson or Washington. The details differ from county to county, and some counties do a better job of sharing information than others. The point is that the courthouse is open to you, as it was not to many of the people in this module.
