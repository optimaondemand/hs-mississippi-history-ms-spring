📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Mississippi's legislature has 174 members. Its governor shares the executive branch with seven other statewide elected officials. And the constitution that sets all this up dates from 1890. How does the state you live in actually run?
So far this course has told the story of Mississippi's past. This lesson turns to the machinery of its government today. It asks you to learn how the state is organized, and then to judge whether the plan is a good one.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
bicameral (by-KAM-er-ul) : Having two chambers, or houses, in a lawmaking body.
judiciary (joo-DISH-ee-air-ee) : The branch of government made up of the courts and judges.
separation of powers (sep-uh-RAY-shun uv POW-erz) : The idea that government power should be divided among separate branches so that no one group holds all of it.
veto (VEE-toh) : The power of a chief executive to reject a bill passed by the legislature.
📚 PART 1 · ONE IDEA, THREE BRANCHES

Dividing Power

Every government must solve the same first problems: keeping the peace, making laws, and settling disputes. The question is who should hold the power to do these things. The founders of the United States feared that if one person or group held all of it, liberty would not last. Their answer was the separation of powers. Government would be divided into separate branches, and each would limit the others.

Mississippi's constitution uses the same idea. Article 1 of the Constitution of 1890 is called the Distribution of Powers. It says that the powers of government are divided into three departments: legislative, executive, and judicial. It also says that no person belonging to one department may exercise the powers of another, except where the constitution allows it. You will read it in Primary Source 32.1.

Why does this matter? Think about the Ole Miss crisis. When Governor Barnett tried to block a federal court order, he was trying to use executive power to cancel the work of the courts. If the executive can undo the courts, the courts cannot protect anyone. The separation of powers is the reason a judge's order carries weight against a governor.

James Madison explained the idea in Federalist No. 51, which you will read this week. He argued that people are not angels, so a government must be built to work even when its officers are not. If each branch has its own ambitions, each will guard its own power against the others. The plan does not depend on good luck or on perfect leaders. It depends on structure.

📚 PART 2 · THE LEGISLATURE

The Lawmakers

Mississippi's lawmaking body, the legislature, is bicameral. It has two houses: a Senate with 52 members and a House of Representatives with 122. Members of both houses serve four-year terms. They meet in Jackson at the State Capitol, which was finished in 1903. The legislature writes the laws. It also sets the budget and decides how to spend the state's tax money.

The Lieutenant Governor presides over the Senate and has great influence there, including the power to choose committee chairs. The House elects its own presiding officer, called the Speaker. When a bill passes both houses, it goes to the governor. The governor may sign it, or may veto it. A veto can be overridden if two thirds of each house vote to do so. In money bills, the governor may also veto particular items, which is called a line-item veto.

A bill starts as an idea. A member introduces it, and a committee studies it. The committee may change it or let it die. If it passes the committee, the whole house votes. Then the other house does the same. Most bills never become law. That slow process is on purpose. It gives members time to ask who will be helped, who will pay, and what could go wrong.

💡 MISSISSIPPI TODAY
An Old Rule, Changed
The 1890 Constitution contained an unusual rule for electing statewide officers. A candidate had to win a majority of the popular vote and also carry a majority of the 122 House districts. If no one did both, the House of Representatives chose the winner. Historians point out that the rule came from the same convention that wrote the poll tax and literacy test, and that it was designed to reduce the power of Black voters. It was rarely tested, but it stayed on the books for more than a century. In 2020, Mississippi voters approved an amendment to change how statewide offices are decided. The story is a good example of how an old rule can remain in a constitution long after the reasons for it have been forgotten, and how citizens can change it.
📚 PART 3 · THE EXECUTIVE

One Governor, Seven Other Officers

In many states, the governor appoints most of the leaders of the executive branch. Mississippi is different. Voters elect eight officers across the whole state: the governor, the lieutenant governor, the secretary of state, the attorney general, the treasurer, the auditor, the commissioner of agriculture and commerce, and the commissioner of insurance. The governor cannot fire any of the others, because they answer to the voters.

The governor serves a four-year term, and is limited to two terms. The governor proposes a budget, signs or vetoes bills, commands the National Guard when it is not in federal service, and appoints many agency heads, with the consent of the Senate. But with so much power given to other officers, the governor of Mississippi has traditionally been called weaker than many of the nation's governors.

The other officers have clear jobs. The attorney general is the state's lawyer. The secretary of state oversees elections and keeps business and land records. The treasurer holds the state's money, and the auditor checks how public money is spent. The commissioner of agriculture and commerce looks after farm and market matters, and the commissioner of insurance oversees insurance companies. Each one is a separate office with its own voters.

Why was the office built this way? After Reconstruction, many Southern states reduced the power of their governors, partly because of what they had seen under the Reconstruction governments. Others say the plan fit an older American belief in dividing power widely. Which view is right? Supporters say that dividing power prevents any one person from controlling the state. Critics say that no one is clearly in charge, and that voters cannot easily tell who to blame when something goes wrong.

📚 PART 4 · THE COURTS

The Judiciary

The state judiciary has several levels. At the top is the Mississippi Supreme Court, with nine justices. Below it is the Court of Appeals, created in 1995 to help with the court's heavy workload. Trial courts include circuit courts, which hear serious crimes and many civil cases, and chancery courts, which handle matters such as wills, land, and divorce. There are also county courts and justice courts for smaller matters.

Mississippi voters elect their judges, and the ballots for judicial races are nonpartisan, meaning that candidates do not run under party labels. The state's courts interpret the state constitution and state laws. Federal courts, which you have seen at work in this module, interpret the national Constitution and federal laws. A case may start in one system and end in the other, as the Meredith case did.

Courts also have a special power. A court may decide that a law passed by the legislature breaks the constitution, and may refuse to enforce it. This is called judicial review. It is a check on the legislature and on the governor. It is also a reason that court orders, like the one in the Meredith case, must be obeyed even by powerful officials.
