📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In the fall of 1963, about eighty thousand Black Mississippians cast ballots in an election that the state would not count. Why vote in a contest that did not count? Their answer shaped the next year.
For most of the twentieth century, the vote was the one right that Black Mississippians were kept from using. This lesson explains how the state blocked them and who kept trying anyway. It asks what it takes to claim a right that your own government denies.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
Council of Federated Organizations (COFO) (KOWN-sul uv FED-er-ay-tid or-guh-nih-ZAY-shunz) : A group formed in 1962 to unite the main civil rights organizations working in Mississippi. People called it COFO (KOH-foh).
Freedom Vote (FREE-dum voht) : A mock election held in November 1963 in which Black Mississippians cast ballots to show that they wanted to vote.
literacy test (LIT-er-uh-see test) : A test of reading or understanding given to people who wanted to register to vote. In Mississippi, a registrar judged whether an answer was good enough.
SNCC (SNIK) : The Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, a student-led civil rights group formed in 1960.
Sovereignty Commission (SOV-run-tee kuh-MISH-un) : A state agency created by the Mississippi legislature in 1956 to defend segregation and what it called the state's rights. It spied on civil rights workers.
📚 PART 1 · A DOOR KEPT SHUT

Why So Few Could Vote

In 1870, Black men voted in large numbers in Mississippi, and Hiram Revels took a seat in the United States Senate. The Constitution of 1890, which you studied in Module 25, changed that. It set up a poll tax, and it required would-be voters to pass a literacy test in which they had to read and explain a section of the state constitution. A white registrar decided whether the answer was good enough. White applicants were often passed, and Black applicants, even teachers and ministers, were often failed.

By 1964, only about seven in a hundred Black adults in Mississippi were registered to vote. A few counties where Black residents were a majority had almost no Black voters. The reasons were not only legal. People who tried to register could lose a job, a loan, or a home. As you saw in Module 28, some paid with their lives. In 1955 the Reverend George Lee and Lamar Smith were killed for working to register voters. The state's Sovereignty Commission also watched many organizers and kept files on them.

The struggle was older than the 1960s. After World War II, Black veterans like Medgar Evers tried to register and were turned away. In the 1950s, Dr. T. R. M. Howard of Mound Bayou led a group that urged Black Mississippians to register and to buy gas and goods from Black-owned businesses. The state also required applicants' names to be published in the local newspaper. Employers and neighbors could see who had applied, and many applicants lost work. Each barrier made the next one easier to defend, and together they kept the rolls nearly all white.

📚 PART 2 · ORGANIZERS ARRIVE

Bob Moses and McComb

In 1961 a young teacher from New York named Robert Moses came to Mississippi at the invitation of Amzie Moore, a Delta NAACP leader. Moses worked with SNCC, a group of student activists. He opened a voter registration school in McComb, in the southwest corner of the state. It was slow, dangerous work. In September 1961 Herbert Lee, a farmer who had helped Moses, was shot and killed in Liberty by E. H. Hurst, a state legislator. A coroner's jury called it self-defense, and no one was punished.

Moses and his coworkers did not leave. They moved north into the Delta, where Black residents were the majority and the need was greatest. In 1962 the main groups at work in Mississippi joined together in the Council of Federated Organizations. Aaron Henry, a Clarksdale druggist and the state NAACP president, became its president, and Moses ran its programs. The groups put aside some rivalries because they all wanted the same thing.

The organizers had to build trust before they could build anything else. They ate with local families, slept in their homes, and listened. They held mass meetings in churches, where people sang and prayed before they talked about registering. The churches were the strongest institutions that Black Mississippians controlled. A church could hold a meeting without asking permission, and its members already trusted one another. The movement worked because it grew from places that people already knew.

💡 MISSISSIPPI HISTORY
Amzie Moore of Cleveland
Amzie Moore was a World War II veteran who lived in Cleveland, in the Delta. He ran a gas station and led the local NAACP. Moore had spent years thinking about how to break the hold of fear on the Delta. He invited young organizers to stay in his home and shared his knowledge of the people and the land. He also kept guns by his door, because the danger was real. Moore understood that a movement needs older, rooted people as well as young volunteers. He helped Moses understand where to work and whom to trust. Many people who joined the movement later said they came because someone like Moore had been quietly working there for years before they arrived. Moore reminds us that great events often depend on ordinary people whose names are less well known.
📚 PART 3 · FANNIE LOU HAMER

A Sharecropper Goes to the Courthouse

Fannie Lou Hamer was the youngest of twenty children in a sharecropping family. She lived near Ruleville, in Sunflower County, and worked on a plantation for eighteen years. In August 1962 she attended a meeting at a church where SNCC workers talked about the right to vote. She had not known that Black people had that right. She volunteered to go to the courthouse the next time they went.

On August 31, 1962, she and others rode a bus to Indianola and tried to register. She was told to interpret a section of the state constitution, and the clerk said she had failed. When she returned to the plantation, the owner told her to withdraw her application or leave. She left. Days later, shots were fired into a house where she was staying.

Hamer did not retreat. She began working for SNCC, and her voice became one of the strongest in the movement. In June 1963, on a trip home from a training session, she and others were arrested in Winona and beaten in the county jail. She carried the injuries for the rest of her life. She kept speaking, and she sang. Her songs gave people strength at meetings across the Delta.

Hamer's power came partly from how she spoke. She talked like the people she was talking to, in plain words, with a preacher's rhythm. She said what had happened to her and let the facts do the work. When she sang old spirituals at meetings, the room joined her. Many who heard her said they felt less alone. That is part of what a leader does: she makes it easier for others to find their own courage.

📚 PART 4 · THE FREEDOM VOTE

A Vote That Did Not Count

White officials often claimed that Black Mississippians did not care about voting. In November 1963 COFO decided to test that claim. It organized a mock election called the Freedom Vote. Aaron Henry ran for governor and the Reverend Edwin King, the chaplain at Tougaloo College, ran for lieutenant governor. Volunteers set up polling places in churches, stores, and barbershops. Several dozen white students from Yale and Stanford came to help.

About eighty thousand people voted. Many had never held a ballot before. They knew that the state would not count the votes. They came anyway, because the mock election let them say in public what they wanted. It also gave the organizers an idea. If so many wanted to vote, perhaps the nation would pay attention if hundreds of volunteers came to Mississippi the next summer.

Think about what the Freedom Vote proved. A claim had been made for years: that Black Mississippians were content and did not want to vote. The Freedom Vote answered that claim with a number. It did not change the law, but it changed the argument. Those who wanted to keep the rolls closed could no longer say that no one wanted to open them.
