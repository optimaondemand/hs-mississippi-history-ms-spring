📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
On August 22, 1964, a Mississippi sharecropper testified on national television. President Lyndon Johnson hurriedly called a press conference, which many people understood as an attempt to draw the cameras away. What did she say that was so hard to ignore, and what happened next?
In the summer of 1964, hundreds of volunteers came to Mississippi, and three of them were murdered. Black Mississippians then carried their case to a national political convention. This lesson follows that summer and asks whether its outcome was a defeat or the start of something larger.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
Council of Federated Organizations (COFO) (KOWN-sul uv FED-er-ay-tid or-guh-nih-ZAY-shunz): A group formed in 1962 to unite the main civil rights organizations working in Mississippi. People called it COFO (KOH-foh).
credentials committee (kruh-DEN-shulz kuh-MIT-ee): The group at a party convention that decides which delegates have the right to take part.
delegate (DEL-uh-git): A person chosen to represent others at a convention and to vote for them.
Freedom School (FREE-dum skool): A school run in the summer of 1964 in churches and homes to teach Black students reading, history, and citizenship.
Freedom Summer (FREE-dum SUM-er): The summer of 1964, when hundreds of volunteers came to Mississippi to help Black residents register to vote and to teach in Freedom Schools.
Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party (MFDP) (mis-ih-SIP-ee FREE-dum dem-uh-KRAT-ik PAR-tee): A party formed in 1964 by Black Mississippians and their allies, who had been shut out of the state's regular Democratic Party. People called it the MFDP.
📚 PART 1 · THE SUMMER PLAN

Freedom Summer

After the Freedom Vote, the Council of Federated Organizations planned a bold project for the summer of 1964. It invited hundreds of college students, many of them white and from the North, to come to Mississippi. Some organizers worried about this idea. They feared that local people might be overshadowed, and that the volunteers would be in danger. Others argued that the nation paid more attention when white students were at risk. The plan went ahead and became known as Freedom Summer.

In June, volunteers trained in Ohio and then drove south. About a thousand came in all. They lived with Black families who took them in, even though doing so was dangerous. Some volunteers helped people try to register. Others taught in Freedom Schools, which met in churches and on porches and taught reading, history, and how government works. The students asked questions that their regular schools had never allowed. Many left Freedom School convinced that they, too, could help shape their state.

The volunteers had training before they went. At a college in Ohio they learned how to stay calm when threatened, how to protect themselves without fighting back, and how to listen to the people they would meet. Veteran organizers told them plainly that the summer might cost lives. Some volunteers went home early. Most stayed, and many later said the summer changed their lives.

The response was violent. Dozens of churches and homes were burned or bombed that summer. Volunteers and local people were beaten, and some were arrested on false charges. The danger was constant, and the state's leaders did little to end it.

📚 PART 2 · NESHOBA COUNTY

Three Missing Men

On June 21, 1964, three young men set out from Meridian to investigate a church burning near Philadelphia, in Neshoba County. They were James Chaney, a Black Mississippian and local organizer, and Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner, two white volunteers from New York. A deputy sheriff arrested them, jailed them, and released them that night. They were then stopped by a group of Klansmen and murdered.

President Johnson sent federal agents, and the nation watched. The FBI opened an office in Jackson. Six weeks later, on August 4, agents found the bodies buried in an earthen dam. The state did not bring murder charges. In 1967 a federal court convicted seven men of conspiracy to violate the victims' civil rights. They included a deputy sheriff and the leader of a Klan group. Edgar Ray Killen was tried by the state in 2005, forty-one years later, and convicted of manslaughter.

The murders did not end the summer, but they changed how the nation saw it. Congress had passed the Civil Rights Act just weeks before, in early July. The Neshoba case showed that a law on paper did not protect people in Mississippi unless someone enforced it. It also drew national attention to what Black Mississippians had been enduring for years, and some Mississippians began to speak out. Chaney's family and friends reminded the public that he had been a Mississippian who gave his life for his neighbors.

📚 PART 3 · ATLANTIC CITY

A Party of Their Own

That spring, Black Mississippians and their allies formed the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party. The state's regular Democratic Party had kept Black citizens out of its meetings. So the new party held its own meetings in precincts, counties, and a state convention. It chose sixty-eight delegates to go to the national Democratic convention in Atlantic City, New Jersey. They asked the party to seat them in place of the all-white regular delegation.

On August 22, the credentials committee heard from them. Fannie Lou Hamer told the story of her arrest and beating in Winona. She closed by asking whether America could call itself free when its citizens were treated this way. Her testimony was shown on television. President Johnson, who wanted to win the election and keep Southern white support, did not want the matter to grow.

Party leaders offered a compromise. The Freedom Democrats would receive two seats as at-large guests, and the regular delegates would be seated if they signed a pledge of loyalty to the national ticket. The party also promised that no state delegation chosen in a discriminatory way would be seated in 1968. Some leaders urged the Freedom Democrats to accept. Hamer and most of the delegation refused. They had come to be seated as the real delegation from Mississippi, and two seats would not do that.

📚 PART 4 · FAILURE OR BEGINNING?

What Came After

At the time, many in the Freedom Democratic Party felt betrayed. They had followed the rules, told the truth, and still been turned away. Some young organizers lost faith that the national party would ever act on its promises. Others said the testimony changed the debate.

In 1968, the Democratic convention did exactly what had been promised four years before. It refused to seat Mississippi's all-white regular delegation and instead seated a mixed group that included many of the same leaders. In August 1965, Congress passed the Voting Rights Act, which suspended literacy tests and allowed federal examiners to register voters. In Mississippi, Black registration rose from about seven percent in 1964 to about six in ten by 1967.

Which of these accounts is right? The delegates did not win what they came for, and some of the young people who traveled to Atlantic City never trusted party politics again. Yet the testimony reached millions of people, and the rules changed because they asked. In the discussion, you will weigh these facts and take a position. Consider what you mean by a failure, and what you mean by a beginning.

Hamer did not give up. In 1965 the Freedom Democrats challenged the seating of Mississippi's five members of Congress, and the House kept them. Later she started the Freedom Farm Cooperative, which helped poor families raise food and own land. She died in 1977. Her life is an example of how people can keep working after a defeat, and many historians see it as evidence that a beginning does not always look like a victory.

💡 MISSISSIPPI TODAY
Where Visitors Learn the Story
Jackson is home to the Mississippi Civil Rights Museum, which opened in 2017 next to the Museum of Mississippi History. Its galleries cover the events of this module, including the voter registration drives and Freedom Summer. In Neshoba County, markers and memorials commemorate Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner, and local residents have worked with visitors to tell the story. Opinions about how to remember these events still vary. Some Mississippians see the markers as an important act of truth-telling, while others find the subject painful or would rather focus on the present. Many teachers and families visit the museum to learn about the people you are reading about in this module.
