📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In the winter of 1862 and 1863, Ulysses S. Grant failed to take Vicksburg again and again. Then he tried something so risky that some of his own officers begged him not to.
Vicksburg sat on the one stretch of river the Confederacy could not afford to lose. Union commanders tried to take it for months and failed. This lesson shows why the city was so hard to reach, how Grant's early plans collapsed, and how he finally crossed the river below the city in a gamble that could have ended his career.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
battery (BAT-uh-ree): a group of large guns, or cannons, set up together in one place.
bayou (BY-oo): a slow, marshy stream or creek, common in the lowlands of the Deep South.
bluff (bluff): a high, steep bank beside a river or the sea.
cavalry (KAV-ul-ree): soldiers who fight on horseback.
feint (faynt): a fake attack meant to fool the enemy about where the real attack will come.
supply line (suh-PLY line): the route that carries food, ammunition, and other goods from an army's base to its soldiers.
📚 PART 1 · A TOWN ON THE BLUFFS

Why Vicksburg Was So Hard to Take

Vicksburg stood on tall bluffs above a sharp bend in the Mississippi River. Ships had to slow down to make the turn, and Confederate batteries on the heights could fire down on them. Soldiers called the city the Gibraltar of the Confederacy.

The land around the city made things harder for any army that wanted to attack. North of Vicksburg lay the Yazoo Delta, a flat country of swamps, bayous, and thick woods that flooded in the wet months of the year. East of the city, steep ridges and deep gullies cut the ground into a maze. An army could march for days in those places and end up no closer to the bluffs.

Both presidents understood what was at stake. Abraham Lincoln is remembered as calling Vicksburg the key to the war, because whoever held it held the river. Jefferson Davis, who owned land south of the city along the river, knew that losing it would cut the Confederacy in two. The general Davis put in charge, John C. Pemberton, was born in Pennsylvania but chose the Confederate side. He commanded tens of thousands of men, with orders to hold Vicksburg at almost any cost.

📚 PART 2 · A WINTER OF FAILURE

Grant Tries and Tries Again

Grant's first plan was simple. He would march south across Mississippi along the railroad while his friend and fellow general William T. Sherman attacked from the river. In December 1862, both halves went wrong. Confederate cavalry under Earl Van Dorn struck Grant's supply depot at Holly Springs, burned the stores, and forced him to turn back. A few days later, Sherman's men attacked the bluffs north of the city at Chickasaw Bayou and were thrown back with heavy losses.

That winter, Grant tried other ideas. His men dug a canal across a bend in the river in Louisiana, hoping boats could bypass the city's guns. They tried to float gunboats through flooded bayous and creeks to reach the high ground behind Vicksburg. Every attempt failed. Rain and mud swallowed the work, and the Confederates spotted and blocked the routes.

In the North, newspapers called for Grant's removal. Lincoln is said to have answered that he could not spare a man who fought. That story may be a legend, but it matches what Lincoln did: he kept Grant in command.

By the standard this course uses, Grant showed a stable trait that mattered. He did not quit when plans failed, and he did not blame others. That kind of steadiness is a virtue in the old sense, because it helps a leader serve a common cause.

📚 PART 3 · THE GAMBLE

Running the Guns and Crossing the River

Grant's new plan was bold. His army would march down the Louisiana side of the river, far from the Confederate guns. The navy would then run its gunboats and transport ships past the Vicksburg batteries at night to meet him downstream. After that, the army would cross to the Mississippi side, south of Vicksburg, and approach the city from the east and south, where the defenders were not looking.

On the night of April 16, 1863, Admiral David Dixon Porter's gunboats slid down the river with their lights out. Confederate gunners spotted them and lit bonfires, and for hours the sky flashed with cannon fire. Most of the boats made it through, and a week later more transports followed.

While the boats ran the guns, Grant fooled his enemy. Colonel Benjamin Grierson led about 1,700 Union cavalry on a raid from La Grange, Tennessee, through the length of Mississippi to Baton Rouge, Louisiana, tearing up rails and burning supplies. The raid pulled Confederate attention away from the river. Sherman also made a feint at the bluffs north of the city. Pemberton could not tell where the real blow would fall.

The navy first tried to land the army at Grand Gulf, but the Confederate guns there were too strong. Then Grant caught a piece of luck. According to his own memoirs, an enslaved man who came into the Union lines told him about a good road leading inland from a landing called Bruinsburg. On April 30, 1863, Union ships began carrying Grant's soldiers across the river there. By the next day, about 17,000 men stood on Mississippi soil.

📚 PART 4 · CUTTING LOOSE

A Risk Worth Taking?

Once across, Grant made another bold choice. He cut loose from his main supply line and ordered his army to live off the country as it marched, taking food from farms and storehouses. Sherman is said to have thought this too dangerous and to have told Grant so. Grant went ahead anyway. For Mississippi families along the army's path, the cost was real: fields stripped of corn, barns emptied of livestock, and homes left with little to eat.

Keep contingency in mind. Pemberton was confused by Grierson's raid and the feints. Joseph E. Johnston, who commanded in the West, was far away when Grant landed, and the two generals soon disagreed about what to do. A clearer plan or a faster response might have trapped Grant with a river at his back. In the next lesson, you will see how a few weeks of marching changed everything.
