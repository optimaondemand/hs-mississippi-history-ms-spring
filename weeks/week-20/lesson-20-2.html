📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Grant could not take Vicksburg by storm, so he surrounded it and waited. For forty-seven days, an entire city lived under fire.
After a fast march through central Mississippi, Grant pinned Pemberton's army inside Vicksburg. This lesson follows the siege itself: what soldiers and civilians suffered, why no rescue came, and why Pemberton finally surrendered on July 4, 1863. It also raises a hard question you will take up in this module's discussion: could the city have held?

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
battery (BAT-uh-ree): a group of large guns, or cannons, set up together in one place.
bluff (bluff): a high, steep bank beside a river or the sea.
garrison (GAIR-uh-suhn): the soldiers stationed in a fort or town to defend it.
parole (puh-ROHL): a prisoner's promise, in exchange for release, not to fight again until officially exchanged.
siege (seej): a long effort to capture a place by surrounding it and cutting off its supplies.
supply line (suh-PLY line): the route that carries food, ammunition, and other goods from an army's base to its soldiers.
📚 PART 1 · THREE WEEKS THAT DECIDED IT

From Bruinsburg to the Trenches

After the landing, Grant won a fight at Port Gibson on May 1 and then did something his enemies did not expect. He turned northeast, away from Vicksburg, toward Jackson, where Johnston had begun to gather troops. His men beat the Confederates at Raymond on May 12 and took Jackson on May 14. Sherman's soldiers burned much of the city's factory district before the army moved on.

Grant then turned west. On May 16, at Champion Hill, he met Pemberton's army in the largest battle of the campaign and won it. The Confederates fell back across the Big Black River and into the defenses of Vicksburg. In less than three weeks, Grant's army had marched roughly 180 miles and won five battles while cut off from its main supplies.

Look at what that march meant in practice. Grant had left his main supply line behind, and he had to keep his army fed from the countryside. Each Confederate battery that did not fire on his column, and each bridge that was not burned in time, gave him another day. A few mistakes by the defenders turned a risky plan into a winning one. A few more by Grant could have turned it into a disaster.

Grant hoped to finish things at once. His soldiers attacked the Confederate lines on May 19 and again on May 22. Both attacks failed, and the second cost the Union thousands of men. Grant decided that he would not throw away more lives. He would starve the city out.

📚 PART 2 · LIFE UNDER SIEGE

Soldiers, Civilians, and Caves

A siege is a waiting game. Grant's army, which grew to more than seventy thousand men, dug trenches that crept closer to the Confederate lines each day. Union cannon and gunboats shelled the city day and night. Inside, the garrison of about thirty thousand soldiers watched its food and ammunition shrink.

The fighting did not stop while the armies waited. Union engineers dug a tunnel under one of the Confederate forts and exploded a mine there on June 25. Union soldiers rushed in, but the Confederates held the breach. Heavy guns, many of them set in new batteries on the ridges, pounded the city. Sharpshooters on both sides picked off anyone who showed his head above the trenches. In some quiet stretches, soldiers shouted across the lines and traded coffee for tobacco.

Thousands of civilians were trapped inside as well. Many dug caves into the soft hillsides, and some families hung curtains and set out rugs. One of them, Mary Ann Loughborough, whose husband was a Confederate officer, lived in a cave with her young daughter and later wrote a book about it. You will read part of her account in this module's primary source.

As weeks passed, rations fell. By many accounts, soldiers and civilians ate mule meat and bread made from ground field peas. Illness spread among men who were hungry and exhausted. Enslaved people were in the city too. Some were forced to dig Confederate trenches and to work under the same shelling, and others slipped away into Union lines whenever they could.

📚 PART 3 · NO RESCUE

Johnston, Pemberton, and the Fourth of July

Pemberton hoped Johnston would bring an army to save him. Johnston gathered a force east of the Big Black River, but he believed it was too small to break Grant's lines, and he never attacked. Pemberton, for his part, felt bound by orders to hold the city. The two men never agreed on a plan, and no relief arrived. That was a failure of command as much as a failure of strength.

On July 3, Pemberton asked Grant for terms. Grant first demanded surrender without conditions, then agreed to parole the garrison, which meant sending the soldiers home on a promise not to fight again until exchanged. It was practical. Feeding and moving nearly thirty thousand prisoners would have strained his own army. The surrender took place on July 4, 1863, one day after the end of the Battle of Gettysburg in Pennsylvania.

Port Hudson, Louisiana, fell five days later, and the Union held the whole river. Texas, Louisiana, and Arkansas were cut off from the rest of the Confederacy. Later that summer, Lincoln wrote that "the Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea." For Mississippi, the cost was heavy. Two armies had stripped much of the countryside, the river trade that carried the state's cotton to market now ran under Union control, and the high bluffs that had made Vicksburg so strong stood in enemy hands. Local tradition holds that Vicksburg did not hold a large Fourth of July celebration for generations afterward, although historians disagree about how strictly that was true.

📚 PART 4 · COULD IT HAVE HELD?

Weighing the Choices

Historians still argue about whether Vicksburg could have held. Some say Pemberton should have abandoned the city while he still had an army to save. Others blame Johnston for refusing to attack Grant from behind. Still others say the Confederacy simply lacked the men and supplies to defend two fronts at once. Each answer assumes a different choice by a different person.

War is the hardest test of a state. A government that cannot coordinate its defense has failed at one of its most basic jobs. The people of Vicksburg showed courage. But courage in individuals could not fix a divided command, and it could not feed a city that was cut off.
