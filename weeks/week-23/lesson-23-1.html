📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1867, more Black men than white men registered to vote in Mississippi. For the first time, a state whose population was more than half Black had a voice in choosing its own government.
The North would not accept the Black Codes. In 1867, Congress took charge of Reconstruction in the South and set new rules for readmission. This lesson follows Mississippi through the Reconstruction Acts, the first elections in which Black men voted, and the writing of the Constitution of 1868, which rewrote the state's laws on voting, rights, and public schools.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
Black Codes (blak kohdz): laws passed in Southern states in 1865 and 1866 that limited the rights and freedom of Black people.
delegate (DEL-uh-git): a person chosen to speak and vote for a larger group at a meeting.
disfranchise (dis-FRAN-chyz): to take away a person's right to vote.
ratify (RAT-uh-fy): to approve an amendment or treaty so that it officially takes effect.
Reconstruction (ree-kuhn-STRUK-shuhn): the period after the Civil War, from 1865 to 1877, when the nation tried to rebuild the South and decide the rights of freed people.
suffrage (SUF-rij): the right to vote.
Union League (YOON-yuhn leeg): a political club, supported by the Republican Party, that organized Black and white Unionist voters in the South after the war.
📚 PART 1 · CONGRESS STEPS IN

A New Plan

Northern voters were angry about the Black Codes and about Southern leaders who seemed unrepentant. In the elections of 1866, they gave Republicans a large majority in Congress. Mississippi's legislature rejected the Fourteenth Amendment in January 1867. That amendment made all people born in the United States citizens and promised them equal protection of the laws. Almost every other Southern state rejected it too.

In March 1867, Congress passed the first Reconstruction Act over President Johnson's veto. It set aside the governments created under Johnson's plan. It divided the South, except for Tennessee, into five military districts. Mississippi and Arkansas made up the Fourth, under General Edward O. C. Ord. To return to the Union, each state had to write a new constitution, allow Black men to vote, and ratify the Fourteenth Amendment.

Opinion among white Mississippians was divided. Some Unionists and former Whigs, especially in the northeastern hills, were willing to work with Congress. Most white Democrats saw the Acts as an attack on the state's right to govern itself.

None of this was certain. The Republicans in Congress were not all of one mind. Some wanted to move fast and some wanted to go slow. When Johnson clashed with them, the House impeached him in early 1868, and the Senate fell one vote short of removing him. A different vote might have changed the whole course of Reconstruction.

📚 PART 2 · THE FIRST BLACK VOTERS

Registering a New Electorate

In the summer and autumn of 1867, army officers set up registration boards across Mississippi. When they finished, about 60,000 Black men had registered, compared with about 46,000 white men. Black Mississippians made up more than half of the state's people, and now they made up most of its voters as well. Registering was not easy. Many freedmen lived far from the registrars, could not leave their work for a day, or feared what their employers might do. Those who registered anyway took a real risk.

The Union League and Black churches helped organize them. Men who had been enslaved only a few years before gathered in churches and schoolhouses to learn how the new system worked, listen to speakers, and decide how to vote. Most chose the Republican Party. It was a party of Black voters, white Unionists from the hill counties, and some Northerners who had moved south after the war.

White opposition was strong. Many white Democrats urged their neighbors to stay away from the polls, hoping to defeat the new constitution by boycott. Some employers threatened to fire Black workers who voted Republican. In 1868, groups like the Ku Klux Klan began to use violence to scare voters and party leaders. You will see more of that in the next module.

📚 PART 3 · THE CONSTITUTION OF 1868

A Convention in Jackson

The state constitutional convention met in Jackson in January 1868 and worked for more than four months. Of the roughly one hundred delegates, about seventeen were Black, and most of the rest were white Republicans, some from the South and some from the North. Opponents mocked it as the Black and Tan convention. They meant it as an insult, but the convention did what no earlier Mississippi convention had done: it included Black voices in writing the state's highest law.

The new constitution gave the vote to all adult men, whatever their race. It removed property requirements for holding office. It set up the state's first system of free public schools for all children. Like the United States Constitution, it divided power among three branches of government and began with a bill of rights. Unlike the original United States Constitution, it addressed equal civil and political rights directly. It also ended slavery in plain terms, which the state's 1865 convention had done only reluctantly.

Parts of the constitution stirred fierce opposition. It barred many former Confederate officeholders from voting or serving, and it required voters to take a loyalty oath. Those clauses led many white voters to reject the document. In June 1868, Mississippi's voters turned it down.

📚 PART 4 · BACK INTO THE UNION

Readmission

The story did not end there. After Ulysses S. Grant was elected president in 1868, Congress allowed Mississippi voters to vote on the constitution again, with the disfranchising clauses taken out and put to a separate vote. In November 1869, the voters approved the constitution and rejected the clauses. The same election chose James L. Alcorn, a planter who had served the Confederacy and then joined the Republicans, as governor. He took office in March 1870, and with him came a legislature in which Black Mississippians held many seats.

The new legislature met in January 1870. It ratified the Fourteenth Amendment and the Fifteenth, which barred states from denying the vote because of race, and chose Hiram Revels to fill a seat in the United States Senate. On February 23, 1870, Congress readmitted Mississippi to the Union. It was the end of military rule, and the start of a harder question.

A constitution is only paper until citizens use it. Voting, serving on juries, and holding office were rights, but they were also responsibilities, and they were new to most of the people who now held them. Whether the new rights would hold depended on whether enough citizens, and enough of their neighbors, would respect them.
