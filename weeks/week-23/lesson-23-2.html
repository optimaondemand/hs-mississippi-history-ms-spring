📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1870, Mississippi sent a Black man to sit in the Senate seat once held by Jefferson Davis. The man was Hiram Revels, and the whole country watched.
The new constitution made a political revolution possible. Within a few years, Black Mississippians held offices from the United States Senate to the county courthouse, and the state began its first system of free public schools. This lesson looks at who they were, what they built, and why their opponents worked so hard to tear it down.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
carpetbagger (KAR-pit-bag-er): an insulting name Southern opponents used for Northerners who moved to the South after the war and joined the Republican Party.
land-grant college (land-grant KOL-ij): a college started with the help of federal land, with a focus on farming and practical skills.
Reconstruction (ree-kuhn-STRUK-shuhn): the period after the Civil War, from 1865 to 1877, when the nation tried to rebuild the South and decide the rights of freed people.
scalawag (SKAL-uh-wag): an insulting name used for white Southerners who supported the Republican Party during Reconstruction.
superintendent (soo-per-in-TEN-dent): an official in charge of running an organization, such as a school system.
📚 PART 1 · A SEAT IN THE SENATE

Hiram Revels

Hiram Revels was born free in North Carolina in 1827, and he was forty-two years old when he arrived in Washington. He studied at Quaker schools and at Knox College in Illinois, became a minister in the African Methodist Episcopal Church, and traveled to teach and preach. During the Civil War, he helped recruit Black soldiers and served as a chaplain. After the war, he settled in Natchez, where he was elected to the city council and then to the state senate.

In January 1870, the Mississippi legislature chose Revels to fill the unfinished Senate term of the seat Jefferson Davis had left in 1861. Some Democrats in Washington objected. They argued that Revels had not been a citizen for the nine years the Constitution required for senators. The Senate voted 48 to 8 to seat him on February 25, 1870. Crowds packed the galleries to watch him take the oath. He served about a year.

📚 PART 2 · OTHER OFFICEHOLDERS

From the Courthouse to Congress

Revels was not alone. John R. Lynch, a young man who had been enslaved and later became a photographer in Natchez, was elected Speaker of the Mississippi House at twenty-five and later served in Congress. Blanche K. Bruce, who had escaped from slavery in Missouri and later served as a sheriff and tax collector in the Delta, was elected to the Senate in 1874. You will hear him speak in the next module. Alexander K. Davis became lieutenant governor. Thomas W. Cardozo served as state superintendent of education. Below them, hundreds of Black men, by some counts more than two hundred, held local offices from school board member to sheriff.

Not every Republican official was Black, and many of the top offices went to white Republicans. Opponents gave them insulting names. A carpetbagger was a Northerner who had come south, and a scalawag was a white Southerner who supported the Republicans. Many were sincere, and some were not. Governor Alcorn, for example, was a Mississippi planter who became a Republican, and the next governor, Adelbert Ames, was a Northerner. You will meet Ames in the next module.

Corruption did exist in some offices, as it did in many states in that era, North and South. But historians now think later writers exaggerated it badly, and used the idea of corrupt rule as an excuse for overthrowing the new governments. What those governments actually did, including building schools and repairing roads, often went unmentioned.

📚 PART 3 · THE FIRST PUBLIC SCHOOLS

Schools for Everyone, in Theory

Before 1870, Mississippi had no statewide system of free public schools. Wealthy families hired tutors or sent their children to private academies. Many poorer white children got little schooling, and the law and custom of slavery had kept enslaved people from learning to read. The Constitution of 1868 ordered the state to build a system open to all children from ages five to twenty-one.

The legislature set up that system in 1870 under the first state superintendent of education, Henry R. Pease, a Northerner who had served in the Union army. Taxes paid for schoolhouses, teachers, and books. Schools were open to all, but in practice most were separate by race. Black communities often built their own schoolhouses, paid teachers with their own savings, and welcomed teachers sent by Northern church groups. Tens of thousands of Black and white children entered public classrooms for the first time. Teachers included Northern missionaries, Black ministers, local freedpeople who had only just learned to read, and a few white Southerners. Classes often met in churches and homes, and adults came to learn as well. The schools were among the most lasting achievements of Reconstruction in Mississippi.

Higher education grew too. In 1871, the state opened Alcorn University, a land-grant college for Black students, and Hiram Revels became its first president. Mission groups founded private colleges such as Rust and Tougaloo. Opposition followed quickly. In several counties, teachers were threatened and schoolhouses were burned. Many white voters resented paying taxes for schools that Black children attended.

📚 PART 4 · WHY DID REVELS MATTER?

What Citizenship Looked Like

Revels held one of the Senate's seats for about a year, and he could not by himself change the laws. Yet he and the others showed what citizenship might look like in a state where most people had been property a few years earlier. As you prepare for this module's discussion, think about the difference between a symbol and a source of power, and ask which one Revels was.

Think also about the people who held local offices. A Black sheriff or county supervisor could break up a mob, collect taxes, or hire a teacher. Offices like these gave Black Mississippians something they had never had before: officials who answered, at least in part, to them. A free people needs educated citizens if it is going to rule itself. A school is often the first public place where neighbors share something, and it ties a community together around a common good. In that sense, the public schools were among the most important parts of the new order. But the new order depended on Black voters being able to vote and on federal support to protect them. In the next module, you will see what happened when both of those were put to the test.
