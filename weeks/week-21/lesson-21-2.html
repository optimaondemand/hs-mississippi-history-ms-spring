📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Nobody asked enslaved Mississippians whether the war was theirs. Thousands answered anyway, by walking toward the Union lines.
More than half of Mississippi's people were enslaved in 1860. This lesson follows what happened to them when the armies came. It looks at how enslaved people took chances to free themselves, how the Union army both used and failed them, why thousands joined the Union army as soldiers, and why slavery fell apart so unevenly across the state.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
contraband (KON-truh-band): in the Civil War, a name Union officers gave to enslaved people who escaped to Union lines, treating them as property of war.
emancipation (ih-man-suh-PAY-shuhn): the act of freeing people from slavery.
freedpeople (FREED-pee-puhl): men, women, and children who had been enslaved and were now free.
regiment (REJ-uh-ment): a large unit of soldiers, usually made up of about a thousand men.
📚 PART 1 · WALKING TOWARD THE LINES

Freedom on the Move

Slavery in Mississippi did not end in a single day. It broke apart wherever Union armies came. When soldiers reached a county, enslaved people left plantations, often in families, carrying what they could. Some brought information that helped the army. According to Grant's own account, an enslaved man helped him find the road from Bruinsburg. Others simply took the first chance to walk away.

Union policy at first was unclear. Union officers called these people contraband, meaning property of war, and put many of them to work. Grant put a chaplain named John Eaton in charge of caring for the freedpeople who gathered around his army. Camps formed near Corinth, Holly Springs, and Vicksburg. The Emancipation Proclamation, which took effect on January 1, 1863, declared enslaved people free in areas still in rebellion. In Mississippi, that promise could only be kept where Union soldiers stood.

Not every Union soldier welcomed the freedpeople. Some treated them kindly. Others stole from them or looked down on them, and early in the war a few officers even sent runaways back to their owners. The army was fighting to save the Union, and its leaders were still deciding what to do about slavery. The people who walked toward the lines helped decide it for them.

Freedom came at a heavy price. The camps were crowded, and food and medicine were short. Disease killed many people who had just escaped slavery. The Union army needed their labor and often did not do enough to protect their health. Even so, thousands chose the risk over staying where they were. Mothers walked miles with small children, and elders who could not make the trip were sometimes left behind with relatives, because staying together was not always possible.

Many freedpeople wanted what any family wants: to stay together, to work for themselves, and to own land. Slavery had broken thousands of families apart through sales, and freedpeople searched for lost children, parents, and spouses in the war's wake. Those searches would continue for years.

📚 PART 2 · DAVIS BEND

A Place Called Davis Bend

Davis Bend was a loop of land in the river south of Vicksburg. It held two plantations: Brierfield, owned by Jefferson Davis, and Hurricane, owned by his older brother Joseph. Joseph Davis ran his plantation in an unusual way, with some self-government for the people he enslaved. One of them, Benjamin Montgomery, learned to read and ran the plantation store.

When Union forces reached the area, the Davis family left. Federal officers turned Davis Bend into a refuge for freedpeople, who farmed the land and, by 1864 and 1865, ran much of their own community. Some raised cotton and sold it for good prices. Davis Bend is an example of what freedpeople might do when given a chance. It is also a reminder that the chance depended on the Union army staying close. You will meet Benjamin Montgomery's family again in this course.

📚 PART 3 · BLACK SOLDIERS AND LABORERS

Taking Up Arms and Shovels

In 1863, the Union army began to enlist Black soldiers on a large scale. General Lorenzo Thomas recruited in the Mississippi Valley in the first half of 1863, and regiments formed in Vicksburg, Natchez, and Corinth. Thousands of Black Mississippians served, by some counts more than 17,000. They fought in battles, guarded railroads and towns, and faced harsher treatment if captured. At first they were paid less than white soldiers. The pay was made equal in 1864.

These soldiers fought for their own freedom and for the freedom of their families. When Black regiments marched through Mississippi towns, many white residents reacted with fear and anger, and many Black residents with joy. A soldier's uniform was a public claim to citizenship.

A Black minister named Hiram Revels, who had been born free in North Carolina, came to Vicksburg in 1864 as a chaplain with Black troops. He helped start a school there. Remember his name, because you will meet him again.

Confederate armies also depended on Black labor, but in a different way. They forced thousands of enslaved men to dig trenches, drive wagons, and cook. These men worked under compulsion. They were not free volunteers.

📚 PART 4 · SLAVERY BREAKS UNEVENLY

Chance, Place, and Power

Many slaveholders tried to keep their workers away from the Union lines. They marched enslaved men, women, and children east into the Piney Woods or hundreds of miles west to Texas. Where Confederate control held, slavery lasted until the war was nearly over, and some enslaved people did not learn they were free for weeks or months. So the end of slavery in Mississippi depended on where a person lived, which army was near, and what choices that person made.

There was nothing automatic about any of this. Freedom came because enslaved people took risks, because the Union army arrived, and because the Confederacy lost the power to hold them. A system that depends on constant force is only as strong as that force. When the force weakened, the system began to give way. The old Mississippi was ending, and the people who understood it first were the ones it had held in bondage. As you prepare for this module's discussion, ask whose war it was, and who fought it without being asked.
