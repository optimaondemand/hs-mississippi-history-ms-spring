Women, Farms, and Hunger

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When Mississippi's men marched off to war, their farms did not stop. Women, children, and enslaved workers kept them running, and the strain grew every month.
The war was not fought only on battlefields. In Mississippi it was also fought in kitchens, cornfields, and county seats, where families struggled to eat and to stay together. This lesson looks at the women who ran farms and cared for the wounded, the shortages that spread across the state, and the anger that grew when many families felt that the war asked more of them than of the rich.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
conscription (kuhn-SKRIP-shuhn) : a government order that requires people to serve in the military; also called the draft.
deserter (dih-ZUR-ter) : a soldier who leaves the army without permission and does not plan to return.
exemption (eg-ZEMP-shuhn) : permission to be excused from a duty that other people must perform.
homespun (HOME-spun) : cloth that is spun and woven at home, not bought from a store or a mill.
impressment (im-PRESS-ment) : the taking of property, supplies, or workers by the government for its own use, often with little say from the owner.
inflation (in-FLAY-shuhn) : a rise in prices that makes money buy less than it used to.
📚 PART 1 · WOMEN TAKE CHARGE

Farms, Hospitals, and Homespun

By 1863, tens of thousands of Mississippi men were away in the army. Left behind were wives, mothers, daughters, older men, children, and enslaved workers. Women ran the planting and the harvest, kept the accounts, and made the decisions that men used to make. On large plantations, mistresses supervised enslaved workers with fewer white men around. On small farms, many women plowed the fields themselves.

Goods the family once bought were harder to find, so women made them. They spun thread and wove homespun cloth, sewed uniforms for their husbands and sons, and knitted socks. Soldiers' aid societies in towns across the state gathered clothing, bandages, and food for the armies. When battles came close, women also nursed the wounded. After Shiloh, hospitals in Corinth and in towns across north Mississippi filled with injured men, and local women carried food and bedding to them.

State officials tried to help. The legislature set aside money for the families of soldiers, and counties bought corn and salt for them, but the amounts were small and could not keep pace with rising prices. Women who had never handled money matters now bargained at markets and wrote to officials on behalf of their households.

On farms with few white men around, enslaved workers slowed their pace, ignored orders, or left altogether, and many mistresses wrote of fear and frustration. The old order of command was weakening even inside the home.

Much of the hardest work fell on enslaved women and men, who had no choice in it. They kept the fields planted, cooked the meals, and nursed the sick, all while the system that held them in bondage began to strain.

📚 PART 2 · HUNGER

Shortages, Money, and Impressment

The Union blockade and the loss of the river made goods scarce. Salt became so hard to find that, by many accounts, families boiled the dirt from old smokehouse floors to get a little back. Without salt, meat spoiled. Cloth, shoes, medicine, and coffee grew rare and costly.

Money lost its value too. The Confederate government paid its bills by printing paper money, and the more it printed, the less each dollar was worth. This is called inflation. By 1863, prices in Mississippi had risen many times over, and a soldier's pay could not buy a family much of anything.

Then came impressment. Confederate officers took horses, mules, wagons, corn, and sometimes enslaved laborers for the army, usually paying a set price far below what the goods would sell for. Later, the government also began collecting a share of each farm's crop as a tax. When Union armies came through, they took food from the same fields. A family might lose its corn to one army in April and to the other in July.

State officials urged planters to grow corn, not cotton, so that people would have something to eat. Some did. Others kept planting cotton because it was how they made money.

📚 PART 3 · A RICH MAN'S WAR?

The Exemption Act and Its Consequences

In April 1862, the Confederate Congress passed a draft law. It required most white men between the ages of eighteen and thirty-five to serve. This was conscription, and it was a new and heavy demand on families who had expected to volunteer when they chose.

Six months later, in October 1862, Congress passed a law that excused certain men from service. One of its exemptions covered one white man on every plantation with twenty or more enslaved people. Leaders argued that someone had to keep order on large plantations and keep the crops growing. Many poorer families saw it differently. To them, it looked as if wealth could buy a way out of the army. A slogan spread across the South: a rich man's war and a poor man's fight.

Desertion grew, especially after Vicksburg, when thousands of paroled soldiers walked home across the state and many never returned to the ranks, and after letters from home told of empty cupboards. Some men simply went home to plant a crop. Others did not hide at all. In Jones County, in the Piney Woods where few families owned enslaved people, a farmer named Newton Knight left the army in 1863 and joined a band of deserters and Unionists who hid in the swamps. Later stories about a Free State of Jones made it sound like a separate country. Those stories are exaggerated, but the resistance there was real.

Historians have also found letters from soldiers' wives to state officials. Some asked for corn. Some asked for a husband to be sent home. Some warned that their children were hungry. Each letter shows how far the war had reached into ordinary households.

📚 PART 4 · WHOSE WAR WAS IT?

The Test of a Common Cause

A government that asks its people to fight depends on their sense that they share a common cause. When the burdens of war seem to land unevenly, that sense frays. In this course, a community is judged partly by whether its laws bind its members together or divide them. Exemptions that favored the wealthy did the second.

Hardship does not by itself make people disloyal. Most Mississippi soldiers stayed in the ranks to the end, and many families kept sending food and letters even when they had little to spare. But the resentment was real, and it wore down the bonds the Confederacy needed. As you prepare for this module's discussion, ask whose war this was, and who was never asked.
