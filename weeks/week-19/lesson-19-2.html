📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
For more than a year after secession, most of the fighting was far from Mississippi. Then the Union army came south, and the war found the river.
Mississippi sent tens of thousands of men to the Confederate armies, and most of them fought outside the state at first. But the state's place on the map made it a target. This lesson follows the war from its first months to the end of 1862, and shows why the Mississippi River and the railroads that crossed the state mattered so much to both sides.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
Anaconda Plan (an-uh-KON-duh plan): the Union strategy, proposed in 1861, of squeezing the Confederacy by blockading its ports and taking control of the Mississippi River.
blockade (blok-AYD): the use of warships to keep ships from entering or leaving an enemy's ports.
Confederacy (kuhn-FED-er-uh-see): the government formed in 1861 by the Southern states that left the Union.
junction (JUNK-shuhn): a place where two or more roads or railroad lines cross or meet.
secession (sih-SESH-uhn): a state's formal act of leaving a larger country or union.
📚 PART 1 · MARCHING AWAY

Mississippi Goes to War

When the war began at Fort Sumter in April 1861, Mississippi's governor and legislature moved quickly. Volunteer companies formed in towns and counties across the state, many named for their home places or their local leaders. Within weeks, Mississippi troops were on trains to Pensacola, Florida, and to Virginia. Mississippians fought in the first large battle of the war, at Bull Run, in July 1861.

Most estimates say that about 80,000 white Mississippians served in Confederate armies, a very large share of the state's white men of fighting age. More than 25,000 of them died, many from disease in camp rather than from bullets. The war took one of the heaviest tolls of any state on its families.

Not everyone cheered. Some Mississippians, especially in the hill counties, had voted to wait, and a few never fully accepted the Confederacy. Once the fighting began, though, most communities rallied behind their volunteer companies. Women sewed uniforms and flags, and families sent food and letters to the camps. Enslaved Mississippians watched and listened. Many understood that the fight over the Union touched their own freedom, even though no one asked them.

At the same time, the Union navy began a blockade of Southern ports. Ships carrying Mississippi cotton to Europe had a harder time getting out, and goods the state needed had a harder time getting in. The wealth that had pushed leaders toward secession now started to slip away.

📚 PART 2 · WHY THE RIVER MATTERED

The Anaconda Plan

In 1861, Union General Winfield Scott, the aging commander of the army, proposed a patient plan. Like a snake, the Union would squeeze the Confederacy slowly. The navy would blockade Southern ports, and Union forces would take control of the Mississippi River. Newspapers mocked it as the Anaconda Plan, but its main ideas came to shape the war.

The river mattered because it split the Confederacy in two. If the Union held it, Texas, Louisiana, and Arkansas would be cut off from the rest. The river also carried cotton, soldiers, and supplies. Mississippi touched the river along its entire western edge, so the war could not stay away from it for long.

Railroads mattered too. At Corinth, in the northeast corner of the state, two major lines crossed: the Memphis and Charleston, and the Mobile and Ohio. A railroad junction like this was a place where an army could shift men and supplies in several directions at once. Another line ran across the state through Jackson to Vicksburg, a town on high bluffs above a sharp bend in the river. Guns on those bluffs could control traffic on the water below.

📚 PART 3 · 1862: THE UNION CLOSES IN

Shiloh and Corinth

In early 1862, Union forces under Ulysses S. Grant won victories in Tennessee and pushed south toward the Mississippi line. On April 6, Confederate forces attacked them near Shiloh Church in Tennessee, about twenty miles north of the state. The fight lasted two days and left more than 23,000 men killed, wounded, or missing, more than all of America's earlier wars had cost combined.

The Confederate commander, Albert Sidney Johnston, was killed on the first day. Many historians believe the battle might have turned out differently had he lived, though no one can be sure. After the second day, the Confederates fell back to Corinth. A larger Union army under General Henry Halleck followed slowly, and in late May the Confederate commander, P. G. T. Beauregard, quietly slipped away before Halleck could trap him. The Union took the town, and with it the junction.

The River Falls, Except at Vicksburg

While armies moved in the north, the Union navy fought its way up the river. New Orleans fell in late April 1862, and Memphis fell in June. A fleet under David Farragut sailed up to Vicksburg, and its guns bombarded the city in the early summer. Vicksburg's leaders refused to surrender, and ships alone could not take the bluffs. Natchez had already given way to Union gunboats in May.

That autumn, the Confederates tried to win back what they had lost. Their efforts at Iuka in September and at Corinth in October both failed. At Corinth, on October 3 and 4, General Earl Van Dorn threw his men against the Union defenses, and some of them fought their way into the streets of the town. Union General William Rosecrans held on, and Confederate losses were far heavier than his. The failure left Van Dorn's army battered and the junction firmly in Union hands.

By the end of 1862, Union armies held northern Mississippi, Union gunboats controlled the river north and south, and only two strongholds, Vicksburg and Port Hudson across the line in Louisiana, kept the Confederacy's grip on the water.

📚 PART 4 · A MEASURE OF THE LEADERS

Jefferson Davis as Commander in Chief

War is the hardest test of a government. A regime that cannot defend itself has failed at its most basic job. Jefferson Davis, the Confederacy's president and a former soldier, tried to hold a very long line with limited men and little industry. He picked generals he trusted, and some served the Confederacy well while others did not. His critics said he spread his forces too thin. His defenders said he had no good choices. Historians still argue about it.

Notice that the war did not simply happen to Mississippians. Leaders in Richmond, Jackson, and Washington made choices, and ordinary Mississippians lived with the results.
