📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
On January 9, 1861, eighty-four delegates in Jackson voted to take Mississippi out of the United States. Fifteen voted no.
Most Mississippians in 1860 lived near cotton fields, and the cotton fields ran on enslaved labor. When Abraham Lincoln won the presidency, the state's leaders had to decide whether to stay in the Union or leave it. This lesson looks at the world they lived in, the reasons they gave for secession, the people who wanted to wait, and why the outcome was less certain than it looks today.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
Confederacy (kuhn-FED-er-uh-see): the government formed in 1861 by the Southern states that left the Union.
convention (kuhn-VEN-shuhn): a special meeting of chosen delegates called to make a major decision.
cooperationist (koh-op-uh-RAY-shuh-nist): in 1860 and 1861, a person who wanted Southern states to decide about leaving the Union together, not one by one.
ordinance (OR-duh-nuhns): an official law or order. In this lesson, it is the document that declared Mississippi out of the Union.
secession (sih-SESH-uhn): a state's formal act of leaving a larger country or union.
Unionist (YOON-yuh-nist): a Southerner who wanted to stay loyal to the United States.
📚 PART 1 · THE STATE COTTON BUILT

Cotton, Land, and Enslaved Labor

By 1860, Mississippi had grown fast. Only a few decades earlier, most of it had belonged to the Choctaw and Chickasaw. After their removal in the 1830s, settlers poured in, cleared the rich soil of the Delta and the Black Prairie, and planted cotton. Mississippi cotton sold to mills in England and New England. Some historians rank Mississippi near the top of the country in wealth per free person.

That wealth rested on slavery. In 1860, Mississippi had about 791,000 people. More than 436,000 of them, over half, were enslaved. Only South Carolina had a larger share. Close to half of the white families in the state owned at least one enslaved person, and a small number of planters owned dozens or even hundreds. Enslaved people were also counted as property, and their price made them one of the most valuable forms of wealth in the state.

Slavery was not only an economic system. It shaped the law and the daily habits of the whole state. Laws limited where enslaved people could travel, whom they could meet, and what they could own. White patrols stopped them on the roads at night. An enslaved person could not vote, hold office, or testify against a white person in court.

A republic depends on citizens who rule themselves and share a common good. In Mississippi, more than half the people were ruled by force and had no share in that good. Keep that tension in mind as you read how the state's leaders explained themselves.

📚 PART 2 · THE ELECTION OF 1860

A President Mississippi Did Not Vote For

In the election of November 1860, Abraham Lincoln won the presidency without a single Mississippi vote. His name was not even printed on the ballot there. Most Mississippi voters chose John C. Breckinridge, the candidate of the Southern wing of the Democratic Party.

Lincoln had said many times that he would not touch slavery where it already existed. What he opposed was its spread into the western territories. That sounds like a small thing. To many Mississippi leaders, it was not. They believed slavery had to grow to survive. New land meant new cotton fields, new slave states in the Senate, and a future for planters' sons. A government pledged to stop that growth, they feared, would slowly squeeze slavery until it died.

Moving Toward the Vote

Governor John J. Pettus called the legislature back to Jackson in November, and the legislature called for a convention. Voters elected delegates on December 20, the same day South Carolina voted to leave the Union. The convention opened in Jackson on January 7, 1861. Two days later, delegates voted 84 to 15 to adopt an ordinance of secession. Mississippi became the second state to leave.

The vote of 84 to 15 can make the decision look settled. It was not. The cooperationists argued that Mississippi should wait and act together with the other Southern states, so that it would not stand alone against the federal government. Some Unionists hoped to stay in the Union altogether. Support for waiting was often strongest in the northeastern hills and the pine woods, where fewer families owned enslaved people. Still, the secessionists carried the convention. Had the delegates waited, the story could have gone another way.

📚 PART 3 · THE REASONS THEY GAVE

Mississippi Explains Itself

The convention also published a document giving its reasons. It begins plainly: "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery." It goes on to say that Northern hostility to slavery had left Mississippi with no choice but to submit or leave. It complains about the closing of western territories to slavery, about Northern states that refused to return people who had escaped from slavery, and about Northern newspapers, pulpits, and schools that spoke against it.

Historians sometimes say the war was about "states' rights." Look closely at what this document asks for. It does not defend the right of every state to decide for itself. It complains that Northern states used their own power to block a federal law that protected slavery. The document is mainly about slavery, and it says so.

The leaders also compared themselves to the founders. "For far less cause than this," the document says, "our fathers separated from the Crown of England." Ask yourself whether the comparison holds up. The founders declared that all men are created equal, even though they did not live up to it. Mississippi's leaders were defending an order in which more than half the people had no rights at all. This course judges a community by whether its laws and habits bind its members to a common good. A system that depends on constant force over most of its people is a hard one to defend on those terms.

📚 PART 4 · A SENATOR GOES HOME

Jefferson Davis Leaves Washington

No Mississippian was more famous than Jefferson Davis. He had graduated from West Point, fought at Buena Vista in the Mexican War, and served as Secretary of War. In 1861 he was a United States senator, and he owned a plantation called Brierfield near Vicksburg, where he held enslaved people. Davis had spoken for the Southern cause for years, but in the winter of 1860 and 1861 he also took part in efforts to find a compromise.

After Mississippi left the Union, Davis resigned his Senate seat on January 21, 1861, and gave a farewell speech that listeners described as calm and dignified. He then went home. Mississippi's convention soon named him a major general in the state's army. In February, delegates of the new Confederacy, meeting in Montgomery, Alabama, chose him as their president. By his own later accounts, Davis had hoped to lead armies, not to sit behind a desk.

Every step in this chain, from the election to the convention vote to Davis's own choices, could have gone differently. The people who made those choices were not carried along by history. They decided.
