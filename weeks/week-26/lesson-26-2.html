📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1901 Mississippi opened a prison that was also a farm. It was a very large plantation, run by the state, worked by convicts, and designed to earn a profit. It still operates today.
After 1890, Mississippi's new order took shape in four places: its politics, its prisons, its colleges and churches, and the choice that thousands of families made about whether to stay or go. This lesson tours each one. Then it asks you to think about why Ida B. Wells left and why so many others did not.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
convict leasing (KON-vikt LEE-sing): A system in which a state rented its prisoners to private businesses, which fed and guarded them and kept the value of their work.
Great Migration (grayt my-GRAY-shun): The movement of millions of Black Americans from the rural South to cities in the North, Midwest, and West, mostly between 1910 and 1970.
Jim Crow (jim kroh): The system of laws and customs that separated Black and white Americans in public life, especially in the South. The name came from a mocking character in nineteenth-century stage shows.
lynching (LIN-ching): The killing of a person by a mob, without a trial and outside the law.
primary election (PRY-mair-ee ih-LEK-shun): An election in which members of a political party choose the person who will run for office under that party's name.
📚 PART 1 · ONE PARTY, ONE PRIMARY

The Vote That Counted

After 1890 the Democratic Party held almost every office in Mississippi, and it kept doing so for decades. Because Republicans had almost no chance, the real choice was made earlier, in the Democratic primary election, where party members chose their candidate. Mississippi's Democrats made their primary open only to white voters. Under Jim Crow, politics, like the railroad car, was now separated by race. So the vote that decided who would govern was a vote that Black Mississippians could not cast. In this way the Constitution of 1890 and the party primary worked together. With only one party that counted, officials had little reason to ask Black citizens what they needed. Roads, schools, and courts followed the wishes of the people who could vote.

A new kind of politician rose in this world. James K. Vardaman, elected governor in 1903, spoke for poor white farmers who felt ignored by wealthy planters and town elites. Theodore Bilbo, who later served as governor and senator, did the same. Both men won white votes with harsh racial appeals, and both argued against spending public money on Black schools. Notice what this means for politics. A state that shuts a majority of its people out of voting still has angry political fights, but only among those who are allowed to take part.

📚 PART 2 · PRISON AS BUSINESS

The Pig Law and the Lease

Mississippi's jails filled quickly after the war. In 1876 the legislature passed what Black Mississippians called the Pig Law, which made stealing even a small farm animal a serious felony. The number of convicts grew, and so did the share who were Black. The state had many prisoners and little money to house them.

So it leased convicts to private businesses: planters, railroads, and lumber camps. Under this system of convict leasing, the business fed and guarded the prisoners and kept the value of their labor. The conditions were brutal, and many convicts died. Many Black families lived with the fear that a small charge could send a son or a husband away for years. That fear reached beyond the prison walls and shaped how people lived. Public anger grew, and reformers pushed for change.

In 1901 the state opened Parchman Farm in the Delta, which turned the prison into a state-run cotton plantation of many thousands of acres. Convicts worked in the fields under armed guards, and the state earned money from the crop. Parchman was a different system from leasing, but many historians argue that it kept much of the same logic: cheap labor for the powerful, taken from those with the least say.

📚 PART 3 · COLLEGES, CHURCHES, AND COURTHOUSES

Institutions Built to Last

Not everything in this period was built to divide. Mississippians also built institutions that would last. In 1871 the state opened Alcorn University, the nation's first land-grant college for Black students. Church and missionary groups founded schools such as Tougaloo, Rust, and Jackson State. In 1878 the state created a land-grant college for white students at Starkville, now Mississippi State University. In 1884 it opened the Mississippi Industrial Institute and College in Columbus, among the first state-supported colleges for women in the country.

For Black Mississippians, churches and schools did more than teach and pray. They were places where people could organize, lend each other money, and plan with little interference from white officials. Many later civil rights leaders came out of these institutions, a story you will return to in the next module.

💡 MISSISSIPPI TODAY
The Colleges Still Standing
Alcorn State University, Jackson State University, Tougaloo College, Rust College, and Mississippi State University are all still open today, and several of them were founded in the years you just read about. Their histories remind us that a state can build lasting things even while it practices injustice, and that students at these schools carry that history with them. Many of them hold archives, museums, or collections that anyone can visit or search online.
📚 PART 4 · STAY OR GO

Push and Pull

Between 1910 and 1970, hundreds of thousands of Black Mississippians left the state in what became known as the Great Migration. Historians sort their reasons into two kinds. Push factors drive people out of a place. Pull factors draw people toward another. In Mississippi the push factors included lynching, crushing debt, low wages, poor schools, and closed polls. The pull factors included jobs in Northern factories, higher pay, better schools, and trains that could carry a family north. The Illinois Central Railroad ran straight from Mississippi to Chicago. Letters from relatives, and newspapers passed from hand to hand, carried news of jobs. Once one family went, kin often followed, and the migration kept feeding itself.

Many chose to stay, and their reasons were just as real. Some owned land. Some had family, churches, and neighbors they could not replace. Others could not afford a train ticket. Some believed that Mississippi was their home and that they had as much claim on it as anyone. Staying was not the weaker choice, and leaving was not the braver one. Each took courage, and each cost something.

Think back to Ida B. Wells. She left because staying would have cost her life, and her national work could only be done from outside Mississippi. Compare her with a Delta farm family that had paid for its land and built its own church. Both were deciding what to do about home. In your discussion, ask which pressure mattered most to each, and whether the answer changes how you judge them.
