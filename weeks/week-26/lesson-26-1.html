💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
disfranchise (dis-FRAN-chyz): To take away a person's right to vote.
Jim Crow (jim kroh): The system of laws and customs that separated Black and white Americans in public life, especially in the South. The name came from a mocking character in nineteenth-century stage shows.
lynching (LIN-ching): The killing of a person by a mob, without a trial and outside the law.
segregation (seg-ruh-GAY-shun): The legal and social separation of people by race in places such as schools, trains, and public buildings.
📚 PART 1 · SEPARATE CARS

A Law for the Railroad

After the Constitution of 1890 had begun to disfranchise Black voters, the state turned to daily life. In 1888 Mississippi had already passed a law requiring railroads to provide equal but separate cars for white and Black passengers. It was among the earliest laws of its kind in the South. Laws like this are called Jim Crow laws, a name taken from a mocking stage character of the 1800s. Together they created segregation, the legal separation of people by race in public life.

The separate-car law was one of many. Over the following years, separation spread to waiting rooms, schools, and other public places, sometimes by law and sometimes by custom. Custom could be as strong as law. A Black Mississippian who broke an unwritten rule, such as how to speak to a white person on the street, could be punished by neighbors long before any court was involved. Law and custom held each other up.

The law said the cars would be equal. In practice the cars for Black passengers were often crowded, dirty, and shared with smokers or freight. Black passengers paid the same fare for less. In 1896 the Supreme Court ruled in Plessy v. Ferguson that separate facilities were lawful as long as they were equal. That decision gave laws like Mississippi's a firm legal footing for nearly sixty years.

📚 PART 2 · A GIRL FROM HOLLY SPRINGS

Sixteen and in Charge

Ida Bell Wells was born into slavery in Holly Springs in 1862. After emancipation her father, James, worked as a carpenter and her mother, Elizabeth, as a cook. Her parents valued education, and Ida attended the school at Shaw University, now called Rust College, where her father served as a trustee. In 1878, when Ida was sixteen, yellow fever swept through Holly Springs. Her parents and a baby brother died.

Wells soon learned that her own spirit would not bend to the new rules. In 1884 she bought a first-class ticket on a Tennessee railroad and took a seat in the ladies' car. When the conductor ordered her to move, she refused, and she resisted when he tried to drag her out. She sued the railroad and won in a local court, but Tennessee's highest court reversed the decision in 1887. She learned what many Black citizens learned: a victory in court could be taken back.

Friends suggested that the surviving children be split up among relatives. Ida refused. She claimed to be older than she was, took a teaching job in a country school, and kept the family together. A few years later she moved with some of her sisters to Memphis, where teachers earned better pay. A sixteen-year-old who gives up her own schooling to hold a family together is showing something about character, and you will see that trait again.

📚 PART 3 · MEMPHIS, 1892

The Lynchings at the People's Grocery

In March 1892, three Black men in Memphis, Thomas Moss, Calvin McDowell, and Henry Stewart, were taken from a jail by a mob and killed. A lynching is the killing of a person by a mob without a trial. Moss was a friend of Wells. He and his partners had opened a grocery store that competed with a white-owned store nearby, and a quarrel over business led to a shooting. By then Wells was part owner of a newspaper, and she asked a question that few dared to ask: what was really behind such killings?

Wells traveled and gathered facts from newspapers and interviews. She found that most of the Black men who were killed had never even been accused of the crime that the mob's defenders usually named. Many had been killed for business success, for arguing with a white man, or for trying to vote. She also saw that the usual excuse often hid other motives, such as a wish to keep Black people from competing in business or from voting. She printed what she found in her newspaper, the Memphis Free Speech. In May 1892, while she was traveling in the North, a mob destroyed the newspaper's office and warned her not to come back.

She never lived in the South again. She published her findings in a pamphlet called Southern Horrors in 1892 and lectured in the North and in Britain. She went on to write more and to lead a lifelong campaign against lynching from her new home in Chicago.

💡 MISSISSIPPI HISTORY
Mississippi and the Record of Lynching
Mississippi had more recorded lynchings than any other state. The count kept at Tuskegee Institute lists more than five hundred between 1882 and 1968, and most of the victims were Black. Because these killings were often hidden, ignored, or reported as something else, historians believe any count is probably too low. Wells's habit of counting and checking is the reason we know as much as we do. She taught later historians how to look at the evidence.
📚 PART 4 · WHAT COURAGE LOOKED LIKE

Honesty as a Public Duty

In the classical tradition, a government's first job is to keep the peace and give people a fair hearing in court. A mob that kills a man without a trial is a direct failure of that job, and a community that excuses the mob fails with it. In Mississippi, local officials often looked away, and few mob members were ever charged.

Wells showed virtue in the old sense of the word. Virtue is a stable habit of character that helps a person work for the common good. Honesty and courage were her habits. She did not rely on rumor. She counted, checked, and printed names and dates. She believed that the facts, shown plainly, were the strongest weapon a citizen had. That is a classical idea: a citizen has a duty to tell the truth about public things, even when it costs something.

Her case also raises the question that opens this week's discussion. She left, and her leaving saved her life and made her national work possible. Others stayed, and their staying made Mississippi's Black communities possible. The next lesson looks at the politics, prisons, and institutions that shaped both choices.
