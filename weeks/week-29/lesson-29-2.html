📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
On the evening of June 11, 1963, President Kennedy spoke to the nation about civil rights. A few minutes after midnight, Medgar Evers, the NAACP's field secretary in Mississippi, was shot in his own driveway in Jackson. Why did the state's own government keep a file on a man like him?
Medgar Evers spent nine years traveling Mississippi to help people claim their rights. In 1956 the state created an agency to watch people like him. This lesson tells his story, shows what that agency did, and asks what happens when a government turns its power against its own citizens.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
field secretary (feeld SEK-ruh-tair-ee): A staff member of an organization who travels a region to build local chapters and gather information.
hung jury (hung JOOR-ee): A jury that cannot reach a unanimous decision, so the trial ends without a verdict.
informant (in-FOR-munt): A person who secretly gives information about others to an official group.
sovereignty (SOV-run-tee): The highest power to govern. In this lesson, the claim that a state has the final say over its own affairs.
Sovereignty Commission (SOV-run-tee kuh-MISH-un): A state agency created by the Mississippi legislature in 1956 to defend segregation and what it called the state's rights. It spied on civil rights workers.
surveillance (sur-VAY-luns): Close and secret watching of a person or group.
📚 PART 1 · A SOLDIER COMES HOME

Medgar Evers

Medgar Evers was born in 1925 in Decatur, Mississippi. He served in the Army in Europe during World War II. In 1946 he came home and, with his brother Charles, tried to vote in Decatur. Armed white men turned them away. Evers had fought overseas for a country that would not let him vote at home, and he did not forget it.

He studied at Alcorn College, married Myrlie Beasley, and took a job selling insurance in the Delta. The work took him down dirt roads to Black families in small towns, and he saw how poor they were and how afraid. In 1954 he applied to the law school at the University of Mississippi and was rejected because he was Black. Later that year the NAACP hired him as its first field secretary in Mississippi.

For nearly nine years he drove the state's back roads. He started local chapters, urged people to register to vote, and investigated killings that officials ignored. He helped look into the death of Emmett Till. In 1963 he helped lead a boycott of downtown stores in Jackson, and he spoke on local television about what Black Mississippians wanted. He knew the danger. Friends said he took precautions and kept going anyway.

Evers worked in small ways as well as large ones. He listened to people who were afraid, sat in kitchens and churches, and told them what the law said they could do. He also kept records. When a Black Mississippian was beaten or killed and no one was charged, he wrote down the names, dates, and places. Those notes meant the facts could not easily be denied.

📚 PART 2 · THE DRIVEWAY

June 12, 1963

On June 11, Evers attended an NAACP meeting while the President spoke on television. He returned home after midnight. As he stepped out of his car carrying shirts that read "Jim Crow Must Go," a rifle shot struck him in the back. Myrlie and their children ran to the door. He died at a hospital about an hour later. He was thirty-seven.

The next morning, the newspapers across the country carried the news beside the President's speech. Many Americans saw that the danger described on television the night before was real. Within days, the President sent a civil rights bill to Congress. It became the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Evers did not live to see it pass.

Thousands of people came to his funeral in Jackson. Afterward some of them marched, and police and marchers faced each other in a tense standoff. A Justice Department lawyer named John Doar stepped between them and helped calm the crowd. Evers was buried at Arlington National Cemetery as a veteran.

📚 PART 3 · THE COMMISSION

A State Agency That Watched Its Citizens

In 1956 the Mississippi legislature created the Sovereignty Commission. Its stated purpose was to defend the state's sovereignty against the federal government. In practice, it worked to defend segregation. The governor led it, and it hired investigators and paid informants, some of them Black Mississippians. It also gave public money to the Citizens' Council.

The Commission carried out surveillance on people it considered a threat. Evers had a file. So did many teachers, ministers, students, and NAACP members. Investigators noted license plate numbers, wrote down who attended meetings, and sent reports to state officials. They also tried to keep Black Mississippians from registering and to turn public opinion against the movement. A government that is supposed to protect all of its citizens equally had set up a system to watch some of them for asking for their rights.

Consider how this looked to Evers. When he organized a meeting, someone in the room might be an informant. When he wrote a letter, someone might read it. A free people expects that its government will protect it from spies and thieves. Here a government hired spies to watch its own people. That is why historians treat the Commission as a serious example of a state using its power against the citizens it should have protected.

The Commission also had a public side. It printed pamphlets, paid for speakers, and sent them to other states to argue that Mississippi's way of life was fair. Its leaders said they were protecting the state. Its files tell a different story, because they show that much of its effort went into watching and pressuring Mississippians who had broken no law.

The Commission lasted until the 1970s. Its files were sealed for years. After a lawsuit, they were opened in 1998, and the people they had watched could finally read what had been written about them.

📚 PART 4 · THE TRIALS

Justice Delayed

Police arrested Byron De La Beckwith, a fertilizer salesman from Greenwood, and found a rifle with his fingerprint nearby. He was tried twice in 1964 before all-white juries. Each trial ended with a hung jury, and he went free. During the first trial, Governor Ross Barnett stopped by the courtroom and shook his hand.

Myrlie Evers did not stop working. In the late 1980s a Jackson newspaper reported that the Commission's files showed an investigator had helped check the backgrounds of possible jurors in the 1964 trial. A new prosecution followed. In February 1994, nearly thirty-one years after the killing, a jury convicted Beckwith. He died in prison in 2001.

The long delay left a mark. For thirty years, the family lived with an open wound and a court record that said no one was guilty. Myrlie Evers later became a national leader, and she led the NAACP as its chair. Medgar Evers's brother Charles went on to become a mayor in Mississippi, as you will see in Lesson 31.2. The family's work did not end with Medgar's life.

Think about what this record shows. A government's first duty is to give every person equal protection under law. Here, one agency spied on a man who asked for rights, and a court system let his killer go for three decades. But the record also shows something else. A widow, a free press, and an open archive helped bring the truth out. A community can find the courage to correct its record, even late.

💡 MISSISSIPPI TODAY
Reading the Files
The Sovereignty Commission files are held by the Mississippi Department of Archives and History, and many have been scanned and placed online. Anyone can search them. Some people have found their own families named in the reports, and some have learned for the first time who informed on them. The files can be hard to read, because they are filled with rumors, mistakes, and prejudice as well as facts. Historians use them with care and compare them with other sources. In Jackson, the house where Evers lived has been preserved as a historic site, and the city's airport carries his name. Mississippians still debate how best to remember this period, and the files remain a common starting point for those conversations.
