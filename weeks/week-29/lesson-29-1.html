📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
On the night of September 30, 1962, a Black Air Force veteran waited under guard in a dormitory at the University of Mississippi while a mob fought federal marshals outside. How did one student's application to college bring a state and a nation to the edge of a battle?
James Meredith asked to enroll at Ole Miss, and Governor Ross Barnett swore to stop him. This lesson follows their clash and the two arguments about authority behind it. It also asks what a government owes the people it governs when its officials disagree about whose law comes first.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
contempt (kun-TEMPT) : Willful disobedience of a court's order, which a judge may punish with a fine or jail time.
federal marshal (FED-er-ul MAR-shul) : A law officer of the national government who carries out the orders of the federal courts.
registrar (REJ-ih-strar) : An official who keeps the official list of students at a school or of voters in a county.
sovereignty (SOV-run-tee) : The highest power to govern. In this lesson, the claim that a state has the final say over its own affairs.
📚 PART 1 · THE APPLICANT

James Meredith

James Meredith was born in 1933 near Kosciusko, Mississippi. He served nine years in the Air Force and then studied at Jackson State College. In January 1961 he applied to the University of Mississippi, known as Ole Miss. It was the state's best-known public university, and it had never admitted a Black student. Meredith believed that, as a citizen, he had a right to attend it.

Meredith did not act alone. Medgar Evers of the NAACP, whom you will meet in Lesson 29.2, advised him and helped him find lawyers. He also brought college credits from Jackson State, so on paper he met the university's standards. Still, the decision to apply was his own. He was one man asking his state to treat him the way it treated any other qualified veteran, and he understood that the answer might put his life in danger.

The university turned him down, pointing to problems with his paperwork. Meredith did not give up. With lawyers from the NAACP Legal Defense Fund, led by Constance Baker Motley, he sued. The case took twenty months. In June 1962 a federal appeals court ordered the university to admit him. In September, Justice Hugo Black of the Supreme Court, a former senator from Alabama, refused to block that order. The law was now clear.

State officials still would not accept it. The board that ran the state's colleges named Governor Ross Barnett as the university's registrar, so that he could turn Meredith away in person. Several times that September, Meredith tried to register. Barnett refused him, and later Lieutenant Governor Paul Johnson and state police blocked his way at the campus gate.

📚 PART 2 · TWO CLAIMS ABOUT AUTHORITY

Barnett and Kennedy

Ross Barnett was a Jackson lawyer elected governor in 1959. He had belonged to the Citizens' Council, and he told Mississippians that he would never allow integration at Ole Miss. His argument rested on sovereignty. The Tenth Amendment, he said, leaves powers not given to Washington with the states, and schools are a state matter. In 1956 the legislature had passed a resolution of interposition on the same idea. The Supreme Court had rejected that claim in 1958, but many white Mississippians still held it sincerely.

President John F. Kennedy and his brother, Attorney General Robert Kennedy, held the opposite view. The Constitution says that federal law is supreme. Federal courts had ruled, so the order had to be carried out. They also had reasons to fear using force. They did not want to send soldiers into a Southern state, and they needed Southern votes in Congress. So they spent days on the telephone with Barnett, looking for a way out.

A federal court found Barnett in contempt and threatened a fine for every day he kept defying the order. The two sides looked for a deal that would let Barnett say he had yielded only to force. Meanwhile, hundreds of federal marshals gathered, because the order needed someone to carry it out.

💡 MISSISSIPPI HISTORY
The Taped Calls
President Kennedy had a recording system, and many of his telephone calls with Governor Barnett were saved. Historians still study them because they let us hear two leaders at the moment of decision. In the calls, Barnett insists that he will not give in. At other moments he asks how the matter might end without making him look defeated. Kennedy presses him to keep order and warns that the federal government will act. The calls show how much depended on personal choices. A different promise, a different phrase, or a different hour might have changed what happened next. They also show that both men understood how much was at risk if the crowd got out of hand. You can find recordings of some of the calls through the Kennedy Presidential Library, and you will hear them used in documentaries about this crisis. As you listen, ask yourself what each man wanted most, and what each was willing to give up to get it.
📚 PART 3 · THE NIGHT OF SEPTEMBER 30

A Battle at Oxford

On Sunday, September 30, Meredith was brought to the campus with marshals and placed in Baxter Hall. That evening President Kennedy spoke to the nation on television, and you will read his address next. By then a crowd had formed near the Lyceum, the university's main building. It included students, but also many people from outside Oxford who had driven in. They threw rocks and bottles, then fired guns. The marshals answered with tear gas.

As night fell, the state highway patrol that had been expected to help hold the line withdrew, leaving the marshals to defend the campus alone. More than 150 marshals were hurt. Two people were killed: Paul Guihard, a French reporter, and Ray Gunter, an Oxford man who had come to watch. Near dawn, thousands of army soldiers arrived and brought the fighting to an end. At eight o'clock that morning, Meredith registered.

The marshals were not soldiers. Many came from the Border Patrol and the Bureau of Prisons, and they had been told not to fire unless they had no choice. They held their ground for hours while rocks, bricks, and burning vehicles came at them. Inside Baxter Hall, Meredith waited behind the lines. Whatever happened outside, the order of the court was being carried out one room at a time.

📚 PART 4 · WHAT IT TESTED

The First Duty of a Government

Think about the problem every government must solve first: keeping peace and order under a common law. In September 1962 the state's governor and the national government each claimed the final word. If a governor can cancel a court's order, then no one's rights are safe, because no judge can protect them. If a national government overrides a state without care, local self-rule is weakened. This is why the argument was so serious, and why the stakes reached beyond one student.

Nothing about the outcome was fixed in advance. If Barnett had accepted the ruling in the summer, or if the Kennedys had acted earlier, the violence might never have happened. People made choices, and two of them paid with their lives. Meredith stayed. For the rest of that year he lived under guard and was shunned by many students. He finished his degree in August 1963.

In his address that night, Kennedy appealed to the university's proud history and to the respect for law that he said Mississippians had always shown. He asked the students to let the order stand. Some listened. Others did not. A leader can state a principle, but the people who hear it must still choose whether to follow it.

Barnett left office in January 1964. In your reading and in the discussion this week, you will weigh his argument about authority against the President's. Judge each one fairly, but remember whose right was at the center. It was the right of a citizen to attend a public university that his taxes helped support.
