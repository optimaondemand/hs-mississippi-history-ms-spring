📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In the autumn of 1865, a state with hundreds of thousands of newly freed people had to write its first peacetime laws. What it wrote shocked the North.
The war ended in April and May of 1865, but nobody knew yet what peace would look like. President Andrew Johnson gave Southern states a quick path back into the Union. This lesson follows Mississippi through that summer and autumn: the new government, the refusal to ratify the Thirteenth Amendment, and the Black Codes, a set of laws that limited the freedom of the people who had just been freed.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
amnesty (AM-nuh-stee) : an official pardon, usually for a group of people, for crimes against the government.
Black Codes (blak kohdz) : laws passed in Southern states in 1865 and 1866 that limited the rights and freedom of Black people.
Freedmen's Bureau (FREED-menz BYOOR-oh) : a federal agency, created in 1865, that helped freed people with food, schools, and labor contracts.
provisional (pruh-VIZH-uh-nuhl) : temporary, meant to last only until a permanent arrangement is made.
ratify (RAT-uh-fy) : to approve an amendment or treaty so that it officially takes effect.
vagrancy (VAY-gruh-see) : the crime of being without a home or steady work; in 1865 it was used to arrest Black people who lacked a labor contract.
📚 PART 1 · A QUICK PEACE

Johnson's Plan

Abraham Lincoln was shot in April 1865, and Andrew Johnson became president. Johnson was a Tennessee Democrat who had stayed loyal to the Union. In May, he announced a plan for bringing the Southern states back. Most Confederates could receive amnesty if they swore an oath of loyalty. Top leaders and the wealthiest planters had to apply for pardons one by one. Johnson also appointed a provisional governor for each state. In Mississippi, he chose William L. Sharkey, a judge who had opposed secession.

Johnson asked the states to hold conventions, cancel secession, abolish slavery, and refuse to pay the Confederate debt. He did not ask them to let Black men vote. Mississippi's convention met in Jackson in August 1865. It canceled secession and wrote slavery out of the state constitution. Its members did so reluctantly, and many made clear they were only accepting what the war had already decided.

Many white Mississippians believed that emancipation had been forced on them and that freedpeople would not work without compulsion. Planters complained of a labor shortage as some freedpeople left plantations to test their freedom or search for family. These worries shaped how the legislature approached its work.

In October, voters elected Benjamin G. Humphreys as governor. Humphreys had been a Confederate general, and Johnson pardoned him. The new legislature included many men who had served the Confederacy. In December, the legislature voted not to ratify the Thirteenth Amendment, which ended slavery throughout the nation. Mississippi did not officially ratify it until many years later.

📚 PART 2 · THE BLACK CODES

Freedom With Strings Attached

In November 1865, the new legislature passed a group of laws called the Black Codes. Mississippi was one of the first states to pass them, and its laws were among the harshest. Some provisions gave Black people rights. They could marry legally, own personal property, make contracts, and sue in court. But the same laws took away much of what freedom was supposed to mean.

Black workers had to show a written labor contract, signed each January, or risk arrest for vagrancy. If a worker left a job before the contract ended, he could lose his wages. A freedperson who could not pay a vagrancy fine could be hired out to a white employer. Black people were barred from renting farmland outside of towns, which closed off the path to farming on their own. An apprentice law allowed courts to bind young Black people to white employers, with preference given to former owners. Black Mississippians were also barred from carrying firearms.

County officials enforced these laws, and the men who enforced them were white. Within a few years, the state would also begin leasing prisoners to private employers, a practice you will meet again later in this course.

Together, these laws tried to keep Black workers tied to white landowners without calling it slavery. They solved one first-order problem, a steady supply of labor, by force.

📚 PART 3 · THE RESPONSE

Freedpeople and the Bureau

The Freedmen's Bureau tried to soften the blow. In Mississippi, Bureau agents handed out rations, watched over labor contracts, and helped open schools. They were few in number, and they faced hostility from many white Mississippians. Union soldiers, including Black regiments, remained in the state for a time. Governor Humphreys demanded their removal, and the Black troops were withdrawn within months.

Bureau agents also reported beatings and killings of freedpeople, and they found that local courts often refused to punish the attackers. A law that promised protection on paper meant little when the men enforcing it were unwilling.

Freedpeople did not wait for anyone else to act. They built churches and schools, searched for family members sold away, and met to discuss their future. Many hoped for land. A rumor spread across the South that the government would divide plantations among freedpeople at Christmas. Some white Mississippians feared an uprising and organized armed groups to guard against one.

📚 PART 4 · CHOICE OR ACCIDENT?

Weighing the Evidence

Notice that these were not the acts of an angry mob. A legislature passed them in regular session, a governor signed them, and courts enforced them. A Black Mississippian who had worn a Union uniform could walk past a courthouse and know that the law inside was written to bind him.

Was Mississippi's answer in 1865 a choice or an accident? Part of the answer lies in circumstances. Johnson's quick plan returned power to the men who had led the state before. No Black Mississippian could vote, and no Northern army stood ready to insist on fair laws. Those facts made harsh laws easier to pass. But the Black Codes did not write themselves. Men voted for them, and not every white Mississippian agreed. Other choices were possible.

By the Classical Republican standard used in this course, a government serves the common good when its laws apply fairly to the people it governs. These laws bound one group of citizens to servitude by statute. They failed that test.

Northern newspapers printed summaries of the Mississippi laws, and many readers saw slavery in a new form. The war had been fought, they felt, to end that system, and here it was returning under different names.

The North reacted strongly. Congress refused to seat Southern members, passed the Civil Rights Act of 1866 over Johnson's veto, and sent the Fourteenth Amendment to the states. Mississippi rejected it. What happened next, you will see in the coming modules.
