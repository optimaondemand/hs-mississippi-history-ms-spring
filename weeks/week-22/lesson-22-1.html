The Last Years of the Confederacy in Mississippi

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In February 1864, a Union army marched out of Vicksburg and across the middle of Mississippi, tearing up everything that could help the Confederacy. Many historians see it as a rehearsal for something bigger.
After Vicksburg fell, the war did not leave Mississippi. For nearly two more years, raids and marches crossed the state, and the Confederacy slowly lost its power to fight there. This lesson follows Sherman's Meridian Expedition, the Confederate cavalry general who kept Union armies guessing, and the end of the war in Mississippi.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
arsenal (AR-suh-nuhl) : a place where weapons and ammunition are made or stored.
emancipation (ih-man-suh-PAY-shuhn) : the act of freeing people from slavery.
expedition (ek-spuh-DISH-uhn) : a journey or military march made by a group for a specific purpose.
forage (FOR-ij) : to search the countryside for food, especially food for soldiers and their animals.
freedpeople (FREED-pee-puhl) : men, women, and children who had been enslaved and were now free.
regiment (REJ-uh-ment) : a large unit of soldiers, usually made up of about a thousand men.
📚 PART 1 · MARCH TO MERIDIAN

Sherman Cuts the Railroad

After Vicksburg, the Union held the river, but the Confederates still held much of eastern Mississippi. Their strength rested on the railroads. Meridian, near the Alabama line, was a rail junction where two main lines crossed, and the Confederates kept supplies, hospitals, and an arsenal there. As long as Meridian worked, Confederate armies in the West could be fed and moved.

In February 1864, William T. Sherman set out from Vicksburg with about twenty thousand men. They marched across central Mississippi in less than two weeks, and they had to forage as they went. Along the way they tore up railroad track, burned bridges, and wrecked mills and warehouses. Sherman stayed in Meridian for several days. In his report, he said that the town was gone. He meant that its depots, storehouses, and military buildings no longer existed.

For the families in the army's path, the march meant lost corn, burned cribs, and fear. A farmer who watched thousands of soldiers pass could not tell whether his house would stand by evening. The Confederate government had little power left to protect him.

Sherman had hoped for help. A second column of about seven thousand Union cavalry was supposed to ride south from Memphis and join him. It never arrived. A Confederate force turned it back in a battle at Okolona, in the northeast, on February 22. Sherman marched back to Vicksburg without the second column, and the Confederates repaired part of the damage within weeks.

Thousands of enslaved people followed Sherman's columns, hoping for freedom. The Meridian march also showed a way of fighting that Sherman would use again in Georgia later that year. He believed that wrecking an enemy's railroads, farms, and storehouses could end a war as surely as winning battles. Many historians therefore see Meridian as a trial for the march that came after.

📚 PART 2 · FORREST

A Cavalry General Who Kept Union Armies Guessing

The Confederate who turned back that cavalry column was Nathan Bedford Forrest. Born into a poor family in Tennessee, Forrest became a slave trader and planter in Memphis. He joined the army as a private and rose to general without any military education. He was one of the most skilled cavalry commanders of the war. He moved fast, struck hard, and used surprise better than most trained officers.

In June 1864, a Union force of about eight thousand men under Samuel Sturgis marched into northeast Mississippi to keep Forrest away from Sherman's supply lines. At Brice's Crossroads, on June 10, Forrest attacked with fewer men and routed them. Soldiers in Sturgis's army, including Black regiments, suffered heavy losses. Many historians call it Forrest's finest battle.

Union commanders kept trying to catch him. In July 1864, another column marched to Tupelo and fought two days of hard battles against his forces. The Union held the field, but Forrest escaped and kept raiding. The lesson for Union leaders was plain: holding Mississippi's railroads and towns did not mean controlling the countryside.

Forrest's record cannot be told as military skill alone. In April 1864, at Fort Pillow in Tennessee, his troops killed many Black Union soldiers who were trying to surrender. Historians still debate how much blame he personally carries, but the event shaped how the North and Black soldiers saw him. After the war, many historians link him to the early Ku Klux Klan, which you will meet in Module 24.

📚 PART 3 · THE END

Surrender and Capture

Through 1864, Union raids kept the Confederacy off balance in Mississippi. The state government moved from place to place, and its authority faded. In April 1865, Robert E. Lee surrendered his army in Virginia. In Mississippi, the end came a few weeks later. On May 4, Confederate forces under General Richard Taylor surrendered in Alabama. That surrender covered Mississippi, Alabama, and eastern Louisiana.

Confederate soldiers, many of them thin and barefoot, walked home without pay and found fields overgrown and families hungry. For many, the surrender was a relief, and for others it was a bitter end to years of sacrifice. For Black Mississippians, it was the day the last hope of the Confederacy ended, and many left the plantations that week to find family members or to see what freedom would hold.

Union troops arrested Governor Charles Clark that month. Jefferson Davis, who had fled Richmond, was captured in Georgia on May 10. He was held for two years at Fort Monroe in Virginia and released on bail in 1867. The government never put him on trial. The man who had stood in the Senate and said farewell to Washington in January 1861 now sat in a prison cell, and his state lay in ruins.

📚 PART 4 · WHAT WAS LEFT

Counting the Cost

The numbers were stark. Tens of thousands of Mississippi men were dead, and many who came home had lost arms or legs. According to a number often cited, the state spent about one fifth of its 1866 revenue on artificial limbs. Railroads were torn up, levees along the river were damaged, and many farms had been stripped of livestock and fences. Confederate money and bonds were worthless, and many banks failed.

Emancipation had also erased the state's largest form of property without payment to owners. For the freedpeople, freedom came with no land, little money, and almost no schools. Both groups faced the same hard question: what came next? In this course, you judge a state by how well it solves two first-order problems, keeping order and producing enough for people to live well. In 1865, Mississippi faced both problems at once.
