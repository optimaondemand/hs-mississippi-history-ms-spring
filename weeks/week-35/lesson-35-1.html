📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When most people picture Mississippi's history, they picture two groups, white and Black. But for more than a century, other families have crossed oceans to settle in Mississippi, and each found a place that had no clear spot waiting for them. What did these newcomers do, what did the state ask of them, and what did they ask of it?
This lesson follows four groups of newcomers to Mississippi: Chinese, Lebanese, Croatian, and Vietnamese. It asks how each found work, how the state's racial order treated them, and what their stories show about belonging.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
immigrant (IM-ih-grunt) : A person who moves to a new country to live there.
peddler (PED-ler) : A traveling seller who carries goods from place to place, often on foot or by wagon.
refugee (ref-yoo-JEE) : A person who flees their home country to escape war, persecution, or disaster.
📚 PART 1 · THE CHINESE IN THE DELTA

Neither White Nor Black

After the Civil War, some Delta planters wanted workers who would replace the enslaved labor they had lost. In the 1870s a few of them brought Chinese laborers to the Delta, hoping they would work for low pay. It did not turn out as the planters planned. Most of the newcomers soon left the fields. They opened small grocery stores, often in Black neighborhoods, and sold goods on credit to families who could not shop in many white-owned stores.

These shopkeepers worked long hours and sent money to relatives in China. They also faced a state whose laws sorted people into two groups only. Jim Crow, which you studied in Module 26, had a place for whites and a place for Blacks, and it had no clear place for a Chinese family. Chinese Mississippians were often treated as outsiders by both groups, and they made their own way by running family businesses, joining churches, and building close ties with one another.

The conflict came to a head in the 1920s. In Rosedale, in Bolivar County, a nine-year-old girl named Martha Lum was told that she could not attend the white school because she was Chinese. Her father, Gong Lum, sued. The Mississippi Supreme Court sided with the school board, and in 1927 the United States Supreme Court agreed, holding that the state could classify her with the Black children and send her to the Black school. You will read part of that opinion in Primary Source 35.1. The case shows how the law of the time fit people into a system that was never built for them.

📚 PART 2 · LEBANESE AND CROATIANS

Peddlers and Oystermen

Lebanese families began to arrive in Mississippi in the 1880s, coming from a region that was then part of the Ottoman Empire. Many were young men with little money. They went to the Delta and the river towns as peddlers, walking farm roads with packs of cloth, needles, and trinkets that country families could not easily buy. Once they had saved enough, they opened dry-goods stores, brought over their relatives, and built churches. Some of the stores they began are still family businesses.

On the Gulf Coast, families from Croatia, on the Adriatic Sea, found work in fishing and in the canneries that packed oysters and shrimp. The coast reminded them of home, and the trade was a familiar one. Croatian names are still common in coastal towns, and so are the churches and clubs that the families founded to help new arrivals.

Both groups were immigrants who had to learn English, learn local customs, and find a place without much help from the state. Both were sometimes viewed with suspicion by neighbors who did not understand them. And both, over time, became part of the ordinary life of their towns. Their children went to the public schools, took part in civic clubs, and served in the armed forces, including in the Second World War.

📚 PART 3 · THE VIETNAMESE

A Second Home on the Water

A much newer group came after the Vietnam War. When South Vietnam fell in 1975, thousands of Vietnamese fled as refugees. A number of them were settled on the Mississippi Gulf Coast, where Biloxi and nearby towns offered shrimping and seafood work much like what they knew at home. Churches and charities helped sponsor families, and many of the newcomers were Catholic, so they found a church waiting for them.

Their welcome was mixed. Some white shrimpers feared the competition, and in the early years there were disputes and threats. Over time, the Vietnamese community built its own boats, stores, and churches. When Katrina struck in 2005, the storm destroyed many shrimp boats and homes, and the community, like others on the coast, had to rebuild. Its churches and shops became part of the coast's identity again.

Courage of the plainest kind is at work in these stories: leaving a home, learning a language, and working long hours so that children could do better. The stories also show that a state can lose something when newcomers are shut out and can gain something when they are treated as neighbors.

💡 MISSISSIPPI HISTORY
Where the Newcomers Settled
Newcomers did not spread evenly across Mississippi. Chinese and Lebanese families tended to settle in the Delta and in river and railroad towns, where farm people needed stores. Croatian and Vietnamese families settled on the Gulf Coast, where the water offered work. These patterns reflect the old rule of settlement: people go where they can earn a living and where someone they know has already gone. The picture is a general one, and individual families followed many different paths.
📚 PART 4 · WHAT THE STORIES SHOW

The Place Left Over for Newcomers

Put the four stories side by side. The Chinese grocer, the Lebanese peddler, the Croatian oysterman, and the Vietnamese shrimper all came for work. None came to take part in politics, and none arrived with the power to change the laws. Each found a trade that the larger community needed and did not already control. That is a pattern worth noticing. Newcomers often begin in the gaps, doing what no one else has done well or has been willing to do.

The Gong Lum case shows the other side. A state that sorts people into two boxes will treat a third group as a problem to be solved rather than as neighbors to be welcomed. The Supreme Court did not question the sorting. It asked only whether the state could place one more child in a box that was already there. A reader today can see how the system narrowed the choices of everyone it touched, including those it was never designed to describe.

Yet the story is not only about hardship. Families built stores, churches, clubs, and fishing fleets, and their children became teachers, doctors, mayors, and soldiers. These groups gave Mississippi new foods, new words, and new ways of working. The state's history is richer because they stayed, and it asks you to consider how much richer it might have been had they been welcomed sooner.
