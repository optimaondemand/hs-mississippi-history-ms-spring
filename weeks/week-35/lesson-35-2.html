📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Long before there was a Mississippi, the Choctaw lived on this land. In 1830 a treaty sent most of them away. Yet some stayed, and today their tribe is one of the state's largest employers. What does that history teach about belonging, and who counts as a Mississippian now?
This lesson follows the Choctaw from removal to the present and then looks at the newest Mississippians. It asks what tribal self-government means, how it fits into the state and the nation, and what belonging asks of everyone.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
reservation (rez-er-VAY-shun): Land that the federal government has set aside for the use of a Native American tribe.
tribal council (TRY-bul KOWN-sul): The elected body that makes laws and decisions for a tribal government.
tribal sovereignty (TRY-bul SOV-rin-tee): The right of a Native American tribe to govern itself and make its own laws within its lands, under the Constitution and federal law.
📚 PART 1 · THE CHOCTAW STAY

After the Treaty

In September 1830, at Dancing Rabbit Creek, Choctaw leaders signed a treaty giving up their lands in Mississippi, about ten million acres. Under the treaty, most of the Choctaw were to move west to what is now Oklahoma, and in the winter of 1831 and 1832 thousands began that hard journey. Many suffered from cold, hunger, and disease along the way. Their removal was among the first of the removals later known as the Trail of Tears.

Not all of them left. The treaty offered a few the chance to stay and receive land, but the promise was often broken, and many who stayed lost their farms to speculators and neighbors. Those who remained in central Mississippi, in counties such as Neshoba and Leake, lived for generations as poor farmers and sharecroppers, often on the edges of towns, with little help from the state. They kept their language, their families, and their ceremonies, though at a high price.

In 1945 the federal government recognized the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians, and the tribe organized its own government. The tribe's present constitution dates from 1975. You will read part of it in Primary Source 35.2. It sets up a tribal council, an elected chief, and courts, and it describes the rights and duties of tribal members.

📚 PART 2 · SOVEREIGNTY AND WORK

A Government Within a State

The Choctaw have tribal sovereignty. That means the tribe governs itself on its own lands, which form a reservation near Philadelphia and in several other communities. Tribal courts and police handle many matters on those lands, and the state of Mississippi usually cannot tax or regulate the tribe as it can its other citizens. This sovereignty does not make the tribe a separate country. It sits inside the United States and answers to the Constitution and to federal law. It is a kind of government you have not seen elsewhere in this course.

Under Chief Phillip Martin, who was elected in 1979 and led the tribe for many years, the Choctaw built businesses that provided jobs on the reservation. They made products for large companies, and in the 1990s they opened casinos at the Pearl River Resort. Today the tribe is among the largest employers in Mississippi, with workers from many backgrounds, including many who are not Choctaw. Their story is one of patience. It shows how a people who were pushed out and nearly forgotten built an economy from their own labor.

💡 MISSISSIPPI TODAY
The Choctaw Indian Fair
Each July the Choctaw hold their fair at the tribe's lands near Philadelphia. It draws visitors for stickball, a game that Choctaw people have played for centuries, along with dance, food, and crafts. Many Choctaw speak the Choctaw language at home, and the tribe has worked to teach it to children. The details of the fair change from year to year, so check the tribe's own sources if you plan to attend.
📚 PART 3 · THE NEWEST MISSISSIPPIANS

Who Is Coming Now

Since the 1990s, a newer wave of families has come to Mississippi from Mexico and Central America. Many were drawn by jobs in poultry plants in towns such as Forest and Morton, and others came to help rebuild the coast after Katrina. In 2019, federal agents arrested nearly seven hundred workers at several poultry plants in central Mississippi in a single day. The event showed how deeply some towns depended on these workers, and it divided public opinion. Some said the law had to be enforced. Others said families were being torn apart.

The state has also seen smaller groups come, such as Indian Americans who own and manage motels in many towns. Each group has a story of its own, and each faces the old questions that the Chinese, Lebanese, Croatian, and Vietnamese families faced: how to work, how to worship, how to raise children, and how to be accepted.

What does it take to belong? A classical thinker would say that a community holds together through shared law, shared work, and real affection for fellow citizens beyond family ties. Newcomers can offer loyalty, labor, and new skills. Longtime residents can offer fair treatment and patience. When both sides do their part, a community grows stronger, and when either side refuses, it grows weaker.

📚 PART 4 · BELONGING AND THE COMMON GOOD

Citizens, Neighbors, and Strangers

The word belong has two meanings. One is legal. A person belongs to a country if the law makes them a citizen, with the right to vote, to serve on juries, and to hold office. The other is social. A person belongs to a community if neighbors treat them as one of their own. The two do not always go together. You have seen Black Mississippians who were citizens in law and strangers in practice for nearly a century. You have also seen newcomers who were welcomed in the market but not at the school door.

The Choctaw story adds a third meaning, which is belonging to a place before there was a state. The Choctaw did not come to Mississippi. Mississippi came to them. For that reason their claim to belong does not rest on the goodwill of later settlers. It rests on memory, on treaties, and on the fact that they never entirely left. When Mississippians speak of the state's heritage, the Choctaw are not a footnote. They are the first chapter.

In the discussion this week, you will decide what it takes to belong. Draw on the lives you have read about here. Think about the duties of newcomers, the duties of neighbors, and the duty of the law to treat strangers fairly. A community that gets these right can grow into something larger than any one family or group.
