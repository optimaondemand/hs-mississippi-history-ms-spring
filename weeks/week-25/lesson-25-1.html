📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Imagine working a field all year, bringing in the crop, and still owing money when the work is done. For hundreds of thousands of Mississippians after Reconstruction, that was not a nightmare. It was a normal year.
When slavery ended, Mississippi's economy still ran on cotton, and it still needed workers. What changed was the agreement under which people worked. This lesson explains how sharecropping and the crop lien system took shape, how the Delta was turned from swamp into farmland, and why a bargain that began as a compromise became a trap for so many families.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
crop lien (krop leen): A merchant's legal claim on a farmer's future crop. The merchant supplied goods on credit and was paid first when the crop was sold.
Redeemer (ri-DEE-mer): A white Southern Democrat who worked to take control of state government back from the Republicans. The name came from the claim that they would "redeem" the South.
sharecropping (SHAIR-krop-ing): A farming arrangement in which a family works a landowner's land and gives the owner a share of the crop, often half, instead of paying rent in cash.
📚 PART 1 · LAND WITHOUT MONEY

A Problem with Two Sides

In 1865 Mississippi faced a problem with two sides. Landowners had land but almost no cash, because the war had wrecked the banks and the Confederate money they held was worthless. Freed families had labor but almost no land. Many of them hoped the government would give them farms. That hope was real, but it was not met. A law passed in 1866 opened some public land in Mississippi to Black and loyal white settlers, but most of it was poor, and families with no money for tools, seed, or a mule could not use it.

Landowners first tried to hire workers in groups, pay them wages, and put them under overseers, much as before. Freed families disliked this. They wanted to work their own plots, with their own families, away from the watch of an overseer. They wanted control of their own time, and they wanted to see their children grow up as a family and not as a work crew.

Out of this standoff came sharecropping. A landowner gave a family a small piece of land, a cabin, and often a mule and tools. In return the family gave the landowner a share of the crop, commonly half. For the family, it meant a home of its own and some control over the work. For the landowner, it meant steady labor without paying wages in cash. At the start, it looked like a fair bargain to both sides. Hold on to that thought, because the story of what happened next is a story of how a fair-looking bargain went wrong.

📚 PART 2 · THE CROP LIEN

The Store and the Ledger

Farm families needed food, clothing, seed, and tools before the crop came in. They had no cash. So they turned to the local merchant, who gave them supplies on credit. The merchant, in turn, took a legal claim on the growing crop, called a crop lien. When the cotton was picked and sold, the merchant was paid first.

Merchants charged higher prices to people who bought on credit, and they added interest. A family might pay far more for the same flour than a customer who paid cash. The merchant also kept the books, so the family had to trust the numbers. If the cotton sold for less than the debt, the family started the next year already in debt. A family that tried to leave its debt behind could find itself in serious trouble with the law.

Cotton made the cycle worse. Merchants wanted cotton because cotton could be sold for cash. So families planted cotton on land that might have grown food. When too many farmers grew cotton, its price fell, and by the mid-1890s it had dropped to only a few cents a pound. Falling prices pushed families to grow even more cotton, hoping to catch up, and that pushed the price down further.

📚 PART 3 · THE NEW DELTA

Swamp Into Cotton Land

The Delta was still mostly forest and swamp after the war. Railroads changed that. In the 1880s and 1890s, rail lines such as the Yazoo and Mississippi Valley Railroad cut through the region, and timber companies cleared the forests. Levee boards, paid for by local taxes and federal help, built levees to hold back the river. Behind them lay some of the richest cotton land in the world.

Who did the clearing? Mostly Black laborers. Many came to the Delta because land was cheap and planters wanted workers badly enough to offer better terms than they could find elsewhere. Some Black families bought their own farms there. By 1900, Black people made up a large majority of the Delta's population. A single plantation could hold hundreds of tenant families, and some were run by managers for owners who lived far away.

The state's leaders after Reconstruction, the Redeemers, were happy with this arrangement. They wanted cotton, railroads, and low taxes, and the Delta offered all three. The people who made the Delta, however, had little say in how it was governed.

📚 PART 4 · JUDGING THE SYSTEM

A Bargain That Became a Trap

How should we judge this system? Classical thinkers gave an economy two jobs: to produce enough, and to share the results well enough that most people could live decently. Mississippi's system produced a great deal of cotton. It did not spread the rewards. Land stayed in a few hands, and the families who did most of the work often ended the year with nothing to show for it.

But do not picture sharecroppers as helpless. Black families made real choices. They chose sharecropping over gang labor. They kept their children in school when they could, built churches, and bought land when prices allowed. Some Delta families became owners. The system was strong, and people pushed against it all the time.

Habits mattered too. A region that puts all its work into one crop, depends on credit, and has few schools or factories has few ways out when prices fall. In the next lesson you will see how the same men who controlled this economy rewrote the state constitution to control its politics.

ONE IDEA TO REMEMBER
A bargain is fair only if both sides can walk away. Sharecropping became a trap when families could not leave their debts.
