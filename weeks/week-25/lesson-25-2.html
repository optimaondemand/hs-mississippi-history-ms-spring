📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Only one Black man sat in the convention that wrote Mississippi's constitution in 1890, and he voted for the plan to take the vote away from most of the people who looked like him. Was he wrong?
In 1890 white Mississippians finished what the violence of 1875 had begun. This time they used law instead of rifles. This lesson explains how the Constitution of 1890 worked, what it did to Black voting, and who chose to support it. Then it asks you to weigh a hard choice.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
constitutional convention (kon-stih-TOO-shuh-nul kun-VEN-shun) : A meeting of elected delegates called to write a new constitution or to rewrite an old one.
disfranchise (dis-FRAN-chyz) : To take away a person's right to vote.
poll tax (POHL taks) : A fee a person must pay before being allowed to vote.
sharecropping (SHAIR-krop-ing) : A farming arrangement in which a family works a landowner's land and gives the owner a share of the crop, often half, instead of paying rent in cash.
understanding clause (un-der-STAN-ding klawz) : A rule in the Mississippi Constitution of 1890 that required a voter to read a section of the constitution, or to explain its meaning, to the satisfaction of a local official.
📚 PART 1 · WHY A NEW CONSTITUTION?

A Cleaner Way to Do It

By 1890 Democrats had controlled Mississippi for fourteen years, but they did not like how they had done it. Winning elections through fraud and violence was messy, and it left a record that Northern newspapers could point to. Mississippi's leaders wanted a way to keep Black voters away from the polls that looked lawful and would last.

Historians point to two worries that pushed them. First, a Republican Congress was discussing a bill in 1890 that would have let the federal government oversee Southern elections. Second, farmers across the South were angry about falling cotton prices, and some white farmers were joining a new political movement. Democratic leaders feared that poor whites and Black voters might one day unite against them. A constitution could end that danger by shrinking the number of voters.

In August 1890, a constitutional convention of 134 delegates met in Jackson. Only one of them was Black: Isaiah T. Montgomery. The rest were white Democrats, many of them lawyers and planters. They did not hide their aim. They said openly that the goal was to keep Black citizens from controlling Mississippi's government, though they knew they had to write it in careful language.

📚 PART 2 · THE RULES

Neutral on Paper

The Fifteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution says that a state may not deny a citizen the vote because of race. So the convention could not simply write that Black men could not vote. Instead, it wrote rules that never mentioned race but would fall hardest on Black citizens.

Article 12 of the new constitution required voters to pay a two-dollar poll tax, a large sum for a tenant who rarely saw cash. This was the world of sharecropping you studied in the last lesson. The article required two years of residence in the state. It barred men convicted of certain crimes, such as theft and forgery, though not others, such as murder. And it required that a voter be able to read any section of the constitution, or to explain its meaning to a registrar's satisfaction. This was called the understanding clause. The registrars were local white officials, and they decided who understood well enough.

Everything depended on how local officials used their power. A white farmer who could not read well might be told he understood the law. A Black teacher might be told he did not. The rule did not have to say race. The registrar's decision did the work.

ONE IDEA TO REMEMBER
A rule can be fair in its words and unfair in its use. The courts asked about the words. History asks about the use.
📚 PART 3 · MONTGOMERY'S VOTE

A Hard Choice

Isaiah Montgomery was born into slavery in 1847 at Davis Bend, on the Mississippi River. After the war he and his father, Benjamin, ran a store and farmed in the same place. In 1887 Isaiah helped found Mound Bayou, an all-Black town in the Delta, on land offered by a railroad. By 1890 he was one of the best-known Black men in the state.

At the convention, Montgomery spoke in favor of the plan to restrict the vote, and he voted for it. He argued that Black Mississippians could not win a fight against white opposition and that peace was worth a price. He hoped that if the violence ended, Black education and property would one day bring Black men back to the polls. Many Black leaders were shocked. Others understood his reasoning.

Historians still debate his choice. Some say he was a realist who saw that Black Mississippians had no power to stop the plan, and that opposing it would only have brought more violence. Others say he gave white Mississippi a Black face to put on a plan designed to shut his own people out. You will argue this out in the discussion. As you do, notice one thing that both sides can agree on: he had no good choices.

📚 PART 4 · THE RESULTS

What the Plan Did

The Constitution of 1890 worked as its writers wanted. In 1892 fewer than ten thousand Black Mississippians were registered to vote, though the state had well over a hundred thousand Black men of voting age. Mississippi was the first state to rewrite its constitution this way, and other Southern states copied its ideas over the next few years. The word for what happened is disfranchise: to take away a person's right to vote.

In 1898 the United States Supreme Court heard Williams v. Mississippi. Henry Williams, a Black man in Greenville, had been convicted of murder by an all-white jury. He argued that the voting rules had kept Black men off the jury lists. The Court, in a unanimous opinion, said the rules did not mention race and that Williams had not proved officials used them unfairly. Williams lost, and the ruling made it easier for other states to copy Mississippi.

A constitution is meant to bind a people into one political community with a shared stake in its laws. The Constitution of 1890 did something different. It gave one group the power to decide who could take part, and it shut out a majority of the state's people. The document is still Mississippi's constitution today, though it has been amended many times, and you will return to it when you study state government later in this course. Black Mississippians did not stop trying. A few kept registering, and Black churches and schools kept teaching what citizenship meant. The vote was lost for decades, but the memory of it was not.
