📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1955, three people were killed in Mississippi for reasons that had nothing to do with crime. Two of the cases never reached a trial. The third went to trial and ended in a verdict that shocked the nation.
In 1955, three killings in Mississippi showed how far some people were willing to go to protect segregation, and how a single photograph could change the nation's view. This lesson tells their stories with care. It also asks what a court owes a victim, and what happens when a court fails.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
acquit (uh-KWIT) : To declare, after a trial, that a person is not guilty of a crime.
Citizens' Council (SIT-ih-zenz KOWN-sul) : A group of white community leaders, first formed in Indianola in 1954, that used economic and social pressure to resist school desegregation.
double jeopardy (DUB-ul JEP-er-dee) : The rule that a person who has been tried and found not guilty of a crime cannot be tried for it again.
grand jury (grand JOOR-ee) : A group of citizens who decide whether there is enough evidence to bring formal criminal charges against someone.
NAACP (en-double-A-see-PEE) : The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, a civil rights organization founded in 1909 that worked through the courts and through local chapters.
📚 PART 1 · THE VOTE

Lee and Smith

The Reverend George W. Lee of Belzoni was a minister, a printer, and a leader in the NAACP. He was one of the few Black residents of Humphreys County who had registered to vote, and he urged others to do the same. Local leaders of the Citizens' Council told him to take his name off the voting rolls. He refused. On May 7, 1955, he was shot while driving and died. The sheriff suggested that the lead in his face might have been dental fillings. No one was charged.

Why were voters targeted? A vote was power. In counties where Black residents were a majority, even a small number of registered voters could change who held local offices. That is why some white leaders treated each new registration as a threat, and it is why Black Mississippians like Lee and Smith treated registration as a duty.

On August 13, 1955, Lamar Smith, a Black farmer and voting activist, was shot in broad daylight on the courthouse lawn in Brookhaven, in front of witnesses. He had been helping Black voters cast absentee ballots in a primary election. Police arrested a suspect, but a grand jury refused to bring charges. In both cases, local officials declined to charge anyone, and the message to Black voters was plain.

📚 PART 2 · MONEY, MISSISSIPPI

Emmett Till

Emmett Till was fourteen, a boy from Chicago. In late August 1955 he traveled to Money, Mississippi, to visit his great-uncle Moses Wright, a sharecropper and preacher. Chicago did not have Mississippi's laws of segregation, and Emmett did not know Mississippi's unwritten rules. On August 24 he went into Bryant's Grocery with his cousins and had a brief exchange with Carolyn Bryant, a young white woman who worked the counter. Accounts of that encounter differ. What is not in dispute is what followed.

Early on the morning of August 28, Roy Bryant, Carolyn's husband, and his half brother J. W. Milam came to Moses Wright's cabin, took Emmett away at gunpoint, and later killed him. His body was found in the Tallahatchie River three days later. His great-uncle could recognize him only by a ring he wore. Wright was sixty-four years old. He had lived in Mississippi all his life, and he knew the danger he faced in telling the truth.

Emmett's mother, Mamie Till-Mobley, brought his body home to Chicago and insisted on an open casket, so that the world could see what had been done to her son. Tens of thousands of people filed past in Chicago, and Jet magazine published the photograph. Many Americans, Black and white, saw for the first time how brutal the system could be. Many people who joined the movement later said that picture helped to shape them.

📚 PART 3 · SUMNER

The Trial

The trial of Bryant and Milam began on September 19, 1955, in Sumner, in Tallahatchie County. Reporters from across the country came. Moses Wright took the witness stand, stood, and pointed at the two men, an act of great courage in that place and time. The jury, all white and all men, deliberated for about an hour and found both men not guilty. They were acquitted. The defense had argued that the body might not be Emmett's at all, and the jury was willing to accept doubt. Many observers outside Mississippi did not accept that explanation.

Because of a rule called double jeopardy, a person cannot be tried twice for the same crime. In January 1956, a magazine interviewed Bryant and Milam, and they described what they had done. They could not be tried again for it. Later investigations, including one by the Justice Department that began in 2004, did not result in new convictions.

💡 MISSISSIPPI TODAY
Remembering Emmett Till
Today the Tallahatchie County Courthouse in Sumner has been restored, and an interpretive center nearby tells the story of the trial and of Emmett Till's life. Mississippi has also placed historical markers at sites connected to the case, and some of those markers have been damaged and replaced over the years. Visitors and residents continue to debate how best to remember the case. Many Mississippians now see it as a painful but necessary part of the state's story, while others still find it hard to talk about.
📚 PART 4 · WHAT IT CHANGED

The Weight of One Photograph

Why did Emmett Till's death reach so many people when so many other killings did not? One reason is that his mother refused to let the facts be hidden. Another is the growing reach of magazines, television, and the Black press. A third is that many people could imagine their own children in his place. Emmett's mother also spoke publicly for years afterward, telling audiences across the country what had happened, and she turned private grief into public witness. After the trial, Moses Wright left Mississippi. The killing did not create the movement, but it became part of the story that thousands of people told when they explained why they joined.

Notice the role of the courts. In the Lee and Smith cases, no one was charged. In Till's case, a trial was held, but the verdict said that a Black child's life counted for less than a white man's word. A court that works this way fails the first test of a political community: that its law protects every person equally. The people of Mississippi were then left to decide whether to repair that failure or to defend it.

There is also the question of memory. In the years after 1955, Black families in Mississippi told Till's story to their children as a warning. They also told it as a reason to keep working for change. A community can learn from a wrong in two ways: by hiding it, or by telling it truthfully. Which does a good community choose?
