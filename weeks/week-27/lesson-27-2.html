📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1944, a machine rolled through a cotton field at Clarksdale, Mississippi, and did the work of many pickers. It was the future arriving. For many families who worked that land, it was also the end of the only life they knew.
Between the Great Flood and the end of World War II, Mississippi lived through a depression, a farm revolution, an effort to bring factories to the state, and a world war. This lesson follows those events and asks one question about all of them: who decided where people would go, and who paid when they were moved?

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
mechanization (mek-uh-nuh-ZAY-shun) : The use of machines to do work that people or animals once did.
New Deal (noo deel) : The set of federal programs begun under President Franklin Roosevelt in the 1930s to bring relief to the poor and recovery to the economy during the Great Depression.
subsidy (SUB-suh-dee) : Money that the government pays to an individual or a business to help support an activity.
📚 PART 1 · COTTON FALLS

The Bottom Drops Out

Mississippi entered the Great Depression poorer than almost any other state. When the stock market crashed in 1929, the price of cotton collapsed. It fell from about seventeen cents a pound to about six cents by 1932. Landowners and merchants who had given credit could not collect. Many farm families lost everything. Tenant families who lost their place moved in with relatives, moved to town, or left for Memphis or Chicago.

President Franklin Roosevelt launched the New Deal, a set of programs to bring relief and recovery. One of the first, the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933, paid farmers subsidies for growing less cotton. The goal was to raise the price by cutting supply. The money, however, went to landowners. Many then cut their acres by sending tenants away, and some tenants did not receive the share they were due. A program meant to rescue farmers helped some and pushed others off the land.

New Deal programs also built roads, schools, and power lines across Mississippi. In 1934, Tupelo became the first city to receive electricity from the Tennessee Valley Authority. For a state with few paved roads and little electric power, the New Deal brought changes that people could see.

📚 PART 2 · THE MACHINE AND THE FACTORY

Fewer Hands, New Plants

Tractors and other machines spread slowly across Mississippi farms in these years. The biggest change came in 1944, when mechanical cotton pickers were first put to work on a plantation near Clarksdale. Over the next twenty years, mechanization spread through the Delta. A planter who bought machines needed fewer workers and no longer needed sharecroppers. Over the decades that followed, hundreds of thousands of people left the farms.

State leaders knew that cotton alone could not support Mississippi. In 1936, under Governor Hugh White, the state launched a program called Balance Agriculture With Industry. Towns could issue bonds to build factories and then rent or sell them to companies that agreed to move in. It was an early example of an idea that many states would later copy. Over time it brought garment plants, furniture makers, and other businesses to small towns. The wages were low, but they were wages. For Black families, though, factory work was often closed or limited to the lowest-paid jobs, so the biggest shift in the state's economy did not reach everyone equally.

⚖️ TWO WAYS OF MAKING A LIVING
The Cotton Farm
Work done by hand, season by season.
Pay measured in a share of the crop.
Family and landowner tied together by debt.
The Factory
Work done at a machine, hour by hour.
Pay measured in wages.
Workers tied to an employer, not a landlord.
📚 PART 3 · MISSISSIPPI AT WAR

Training Ground and Shipyard

World War II brought money and people to Mississippi. Camp Shelby, near Hattiesburg, became one of the largest training bases in the country. Japanese American soldiers of the 442nd Regimental Combat Team trained there, in a state where they were strangers. Keesler Field in Biloxi trained airmen, and the Ingalls shipyard in Pascagoula built ships for the Navy.

At home, women took jobs in shipyards and factories, families planted gardens to help the war effort, and young men from small towns saw the wider world for the first time. Many returned with new skills and new ideas about what they were owed.

Roughly a quarter of a million Mississippians served in uniform, and Black soldiers served in separate units. They fought a war for freedom abroad while living under Jim Crow at home. Many came back unwilling to accept that contradiction.

One of them was Medgar Evers, born in Decatur, Mississippi, in 1925. He served in the Army in Europe during the war. When he came home in 1946, he and his brother Charles went to the courthouse to vote and were turned away by armed white men. He would spend the rest of his life working to change that. You will meet him again in the next module.

📚 PART 4 · WHO MOVES, WHO DECIDES

The Question of Force

This week's discussion asks what a state owes the people it moves by force. Look at the pattern. In 1927, refugees were held in camps and made to work. In the 1930s, tenants were moved off the land when landowners cut acres. In the 1940s, young men were drafted and sent across the world. Each case moved people, but under very different rules. The first two moved people mostly against their will and with little protection. The draft moved men by law, and the government was obliged to feed, pay, and care for the soldiers it sent.

A classical thinker would ask what a community owes in return when it asks a member to give up his own choice. A soldier gives up a great deal, and the state owes him pay, care, and honor. A flood victim or an evicted tenant has been moved, too, but the debts owed to them were less clear, and often unpaid. Notice who had the power to demand that those debts be paid. Notice also the question of consent. A person who chooses to leave can bargain for something in return. A person who is held, or who is evicted, cannot.

At the end of the war, Mississippi stood at a turning point. Cotton was no longer king in the old way, new industries were arriving, and thousands of Black veterans had come home with new demands. These pressures prepared the ground for the struggles of the next module.
