📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
On April 21, 1927, the Mississippi River broke through a levee at a place called Mounds Landing, in Bolivar County. Within days, much of the Delta lay under water, and thousands of people were standing on the only dry ground left: the top of the levees.
The Great Flood of 1927 was the worst river flood in American history. In Mississippi it destroyed farms and homes, and it exposed how the state treated its poorest people. It also changed how the nation handled floods. This lesson explains how the river got loose, who suffered most, and what changed afterward.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
crevasse (kruh-VASS) : A break in a levee through which river water pours out onto the land.
floodway (FLUD-way) : A channel or low area built to carry extra river water away from the main river when it rises too high.
levee (LEV-ee) : A wall of earth built along a river to hold back floodwater.
📚 PART 1 · A WET YEAR

How the River Got Loose

The Mississippi River drains water from all or part of thirty-one states and two Canadian provinces. In late 1926, heavy rain fell over the middle of the country and kept falling through the winter and into the next year. By early 1927, the rivers that feed the Mississippi were already full, and then more rain came.

The Delta was especially exposed. It is a flat, low plain between two rivers, built by the same floods that now threatened it. When the Mississippi rose, the water had nowhere to go but out. Along the levees, crews worked day and night piling sandbags and watching for leaks. Many people believed the levees would hold.

For decades, the main way to protect farmland was the levee, an earthen wall built along the riverbank. The federal Mississippi River Commission, created in 1879, had long supported a plan of levees alone. Engineers believed that walls built high and strong enough would hold the river in its channel. But a river that has nowhere to spread must rise, and every new levee pushed the water higher. The system had a flaw built into it.

📚 PART 2 · MOUNDS LANDING

The Break

On April 21, a levee in Bolivar County gave way at Mounds Landing. An opening like this is called a crevasse, and this one was enormous. Water poured through, and a flood many feet deep spread across the Delta. Greenville, the largest town in the region, was soon under water. In places the river was reported to be miles wide. Across the Mississippi Valley, from Illinois to Louisiana, about seven hundred thousand people lost their homes. The official death count was 246, which most historians believe was too low.

Rescue was desperate and uneven. Boats were few, and families waited for days on rooftops with little food. Cattle and mules drowned, and for a farm family the loss of the animals could be as ruinous as the loss of the house.

Many of the people affected were farm families whose crops were gone and whose cabins had washed away. They climbed onto roofs, trees, and levees and waited for boats. Rescuers brought them to the nearest high ground, which was often the levees themselves.

📚 PART 3 · ON THE LEVEE

Who Was Allowed to Leave

Relief camps were set up on high ground. In Greenville, thousands of Black refugees were gathered on the levee, while many white refugees were taken elsewhere. The Red Cross ran the camps, with help from local officials and the National Guard.

Many reports from Black Mississippians describe harsh treatment. Refugees were guarded. Some were not allowed to leave without permission. Black men were put to work on the levees and on relief tasks, sometimes by force and often without pay. The reason was largely economic. Planters feared that if their workers left the Delta, the workers would not come back. After the flood, many did leave.

Think about what a camp meant. A refugee who had lost a home wanted to find relatives, check on land, and start over. A camp that limited movement and demanded work turned a victim into something closer to a prisoner. At the same time, the camps did feed and shelter many people who might otherwise have starved, and some officials worked hard to be fair. Both things were true. Black newspapers such as the Chicago Defender carried reports about the camps to readers far away.

💡 MISSISSIPPI HISTORY
The Colored Advisory Commission
Herbert Hoover, then Secretary of Commerce, led the federal relief effort. He appointed a Colored Advisory Commission, headed by Robert R. Moton, the head of Tuskegee Institute, to look into the camps. The commission found serious abuses. Historians say that Hoover's team softened or held back some of what it found. At the same time, Hoover's handling of the flood made him famous across the nation and helped carry him to the presidency in 1928. Moton's team showed that ordinary citizens can investigate and report on how their government treats the weak. Whether that report changed enough is a question worth asking.
📚 PART 4 · WHAT CHANGED

A Different Kind of River Policy

The flood forced a change of policy. In May 1928, Congress passed the Flood Control Act. For the first time, the federal government accepted responsibility for flood control on the lower river and agreed to pay most of the cost, more than three hundred million dollars. The act ended the old levees-only approach. Engineers added floodways, channels and spillways that carry extra water away from the main river when it rises too high. The levees themselves were also made higher and stronger. The act also reflected a changed idea of what government owes its people. In 1927, President Coolidge had been reluctant to spend federal money on flood victims, and much of the response was left to the Red Cross and local leaders. After the disaster, Congress decided that a flood crossing many states was a national problem that no single state could solve.

The flood changed politics and people too. The state's economy took a heavy blow, and tenant farming became harder to maintain. Black families who had suffered in the camps began leaving in greater numbers. The federal government, which had looked so distant during the 1870s, now had a permanent role in the life of the Delta.

The money problems lasted, too. Many tenants had lost a year's work and were deeper in debt than before, and merchants who had given them credit could not collect. The flood came only two years before the Great Depression, so the state had little time to recover.

The Great Flood also shows how geography and human choices combine. The river was not a plot against Mississippi. But human decisions about levees, camps, and who counted decided who suffered most. A river does not choose sides. People do.
