📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
For a state that has so often been ranked last in lists of income, Mississippi has been first in a remarkable number of ways in the world of letters and art. A novelist from Oxford won the Nobel Prize. A potter in Biloxi was ignored for years and is now celebrated. Why did so much writing and art come from here, and what does the whole course tell you about Mississippi?
This lesson looks at the writers and artists of Mississippi and then draws the course together. It asks what these writers and artists saw in the state, and it ends by asking you to answer one question: why Mississippi?

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
folk art (fohk art) : Art made by people without formal training, often showing the life, beliefs, and traditions of their community.
memoir (MEM-wahr) : A written account of events from the author's own life.
Nobel Prize (noh-BEL prize) : A world-famous award given each year for achievements in fields such as literature, peace, and science.
Yoknapatawpha (yok-nuh-puh-TAW-fuh) : The imaginary Mississippi county, based on the area around Oxford, where William Faulkner set many of his novels.
📚 PART 1 · THE WRITERS

A State That Told Stories

William Faulkner was born in New Albany in 1897 and raised in Oxford. He wrote about an imaginary county, Yoknapatawpha, and filled it with families, farmers, planters, and ghosts of the past. His novels were hard to read, but readers around the world found in them the weight of Southern history. In 1949 he won the Nobel Prize for literature. Faulkner's work shows a historian's truth that the past shapes the present, and that the state's story of slavery and defeat never left its people.

Eudora Welty lived nearly all her life in Jackson. She wrote short stories about ordinary people, with humor and sharp attention to small details. She also took photographs of Mississippians during the Depression. In June 1963, on the night that Medgar Evers was shot, she wrote a story told in the voice of a killer. Her book The Optimist's Daughter won the Pulitzer Prize in 1973. Welty shows that a writer can look closely at one place and make it speak to everyone.

Richard Wright was born near Natchez in 1908. His memoir, Black Boy, told what it was like to grow up under Jim Crow in Mississippi, and his novel Native Son made him one of the most important writers of his time. Wright left the state, as many Black Mississippians did, but he never stopped writing about it. Tennessee Williams, born in Columbus in 1911, wrote plays that carry the voices of the South onto the stage.

More recent writers continue the tradition. Natasha Trethewey, born in Gulfport in 1966, served as the nation's Poet Laureate and wrote about the coast and its memory after Katrina. Jesmyn Ward, who grew up in DeLisle, has twice won the National Book Award for fiction. Their work, like Welty's and Wright's, grows from a particular piece of Mississippi ground.

📚 PART 2 · THE ARTISTS

Seeing the State

Mississippi's artists were as independent as its writers. George Ohr, a potter in Biloxi, made twisted and thin-walled clay pots that his neighbors found strange. He called himself the Mad Potter of Biloxi, and he stored his work in boxes when it did not sell. Decades after his death in 1918, collectors came to see him as a pioneer. A museum in Biloxi now carries his name. His career shows that a great artist is not always recognized by his own time.

Walter Anderson of Ocean Springs painted the birds, plants, and animals of the Gulf Coast. He sometimes rowed a small boat to Horn Island and lived there for days, drawing what he saw. He also helped make pottery at a family workshop and painted murals. Theora Hamblett of Paris, Mississippi, painted her dreams and her visions of the countryside in the style of folk art. Jim Henson, born in Greenville in 1936 and raised partly in Leland, went on to create the Muppets, and Kermit the Frog is sometimes linked to the creek of his Delta boyhood.

What do these artists share? Each looked hard at the place where he or she lived. Each went against the fashions of the day. And each gave Mississippi something that cannot be measured in a table of income.

💡 MISSISSIPPI HISTORY
The River Before the Writers
Long before Faulkner or Welty, Mark Twain gave American readers a Mississippi they could see. In Life on the Mississippi, published in 1883, he described his years as a young steamboat pilot and his return trip along the river. He wrote of the river as something that could be read like a book and that changed its shape from year to year. You will read some of his river chapters in Primary Source 36.1. Twain was not from the state, but he wrote its river into the national imagination, and writers who came after him walked in his wake.
📚 PART 3 · THE CLOSE

Why Mississippi?

You began this course with secession and the war, and you end it with music, books, and art. In between you studied the siege of Vicksburg, the hope and loss of Reconstruction, the Constitution of 1890, the long years of Jim Crow, the Great Flood, the fight for civil rights, the work of government, the changes in the economy, the great storms, and the arrival of newcomers. That is a great deal of history, and it is not all pleasant.

Judge Mississippi by the standard this course has used. Did the state keep order and defend itself? Did it give its people a way to settle disputes fairly? Did it help them prosper? Did it build habits of trust and affection among citizens? The record is mixed. On slavery, on Jim Crow, and on the schools, the state failed many of its people, and the cost lasted for generations. On courage, on endurance, and on the gifts of its music and writing, the state gave much to the nation and to the world.

Contingency applies here too. None of this was fixed. Different choices by planters, legislators, governors, judges, and citizens could have produced a different Mississippi. That is why the people you have met mattered: Hiram Revels, Ida B. Wells, Medgar Evers, Fannie Lou Hamer, William Winter, and many others who are not famous. They show that individual courage and judgment can change what a state becomes.

So why Mississippi? Perhaps because it is a place where the great questions of American life, about freedom, justice, poverty, belonging, and beauty, were asked in their hardest forms. Studying it does not make a person comfortable. It makes a person wiser. In the final discussion, give your own answer, and support it with evidence from everything you have read.
