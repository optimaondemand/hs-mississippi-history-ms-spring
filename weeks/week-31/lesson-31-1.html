📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In June 1966, James Meredith set out to walk across Mississippi alone. On his second day, a man with a shotgun shot him beside the highway. When others finished the march, a new slogan rang out that would divide the movement. Why did some activists begin to lose faith in the old methods?
By the middle of the 1960s, the movement had won laws, but the laws did not yet change daily life in Mississippi. This lesson follows the March Against Fear, the debate over Black Power, a deadly attack in Hattiesburg, and the long struggle over the schools that ended in 1969.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
Black Power (BLAK POW-er): A slogan and idea of the late 1960s that urged Black communities to build their own political, economic, and cultural strength. People disagreed about what it meant.
dual school system (DOO-ul skool SIS-tum): A system with one set of schools for white students and another for Black students.
freedom-of-choice plan (FREE-dum uv CHOYS plan): A school plan that let families choose which school their children attended. In practice, few families chose to cross the color line, and most schools stayed segregated.
segregation academy (seg-rih-GAY-shun uh-KAD-uh-mee): A private school started so that white children could avoid attending desegregated public schools.
📚 PART 1 · THE MARCH AGAINST FEAR

Meredith Walks Again

In June 1966, James Meredith announced a march from Memphis to Jackson, a distance of about 220 miles. He wanted to show Black Mississippians that they did not have to live in fear. On June 6, near Hernando, a man named Aubrey Norvell shot him with a shotgun. Meredith was badly hurt but survived. Norvell pleaded guilty and went to prison.

Civil rights leaders decided to finish the march. Martin Luther King Jr., Stokely Carmichael of SNCC, and Floyd McKissick of CORE joined local people on the road. They camped in churches and fields and signed up thousands of people to vote along the way. On June 26 about fifteen thousand people marched into Jackson. Meredith, who had recovered, joined them for the last stretch.

The march was hard on the road. Marchers walked through summer heat, and local people brought water and food. On June 21, the second anniversary of the Neshoba murders, King led a memorial service in Philadelphia, where officers and onlookers glared at the crowd. Two days later in Canton, state troopers fired tear gas at marchers who tried to set up camp at a school, and many people were hurt. The march showed both the hope and the danger of the time.

📚 PART 2 · BLACK POWER

A Slogan and an Argument

On the night of June 16, in Greenwood, Carmichael was arrested and then released. He told a crowd that Black people needed Black Power. Many in the crowd cheered. Others in the movement were alarmed. Some worried that the phrase would frighten supporters. Others thought it sounded as if Black people wanted to separate from white Americans.

What did it mean? Carmichael and his supporters said it meant that Black communities should gain political strength, elect their own leaders, run their own businesses, and take pride in their history. After years of beatings and broken promises, many young organizers had lost faith in asking for fairness. Critics such as Roy Wilkins of the NAACP feared that the slogan would divide the movement and give its enemies an excuse. Dr. King defended the movement's goals but warned against any message of hatred.

Notice that both sides wanted Black Mississippians to have a voice. They differed on how to get it, and on whom to count on. In Mississippi the argument took place beside daily work: registering voters, running schools, and surviving.

Black Power also showed up in local projects. In 1965 Black Mississippians ran one of the first Head Start programs in the nation, called the Child Development Group of Mississippi. It served thousands of children and hired local teachers. Some state leaders, including Senator John Stennis, attacked the program and worked to cut its money. The fight showed how much it mattered to Black Mississippians to run an institution of their own, and how much some white leaders resisted it.

📚 PART 3 · HATTIESBURG

The Murder of Vernon Dahmer

Danger did not end with new laws. Vernon Dahmer was a businessman and farmer in Hattiesburg and a leader of the local NAACP. After the Voting Rights Act passed, he began collecting poll taxes for his neighbors and allowed people to register at his store, so that those who could not afford the fee could still vote. In January 1966 he announced that he would help even more people.

On the night of January 10, men from the Klan group called the White Knights firebombed his house and fired into it. Dahmer shot back from inside to hold them off while his wife and children escaped through a back window. He was badly burned and died the next day. Several men were charged, but the first trials ended without convictions. The Klan leader Sam Bowers was finally convicted of murder in 1998, thirty-two years later.

Violence like this declined after 1967. The federal trial in the Neshoba case had put Klan leaders in prison, and the FBI had placed informants inside the Klan. Some state leaders began to say publicly that violence was bad for Mississippi. But the damage had been done, and Black Mississippians who registered or petitioned could not be sure of safety even then.

💡 MISSISSIPPI HISTORY
Marian Wright and the Legal Fight
In 1965 Marian Wright became the first Black woman admitted to the Mississippi bar. She opened an office in Jackson for the NAACP Legal Defense Fund, and she took on cases that few lawyers would touch. She represented people arrested in protests, and she worked on voting and school cases across the state. Mississippi was a hard place to practice that kind of law. Judges, clerks, and neighbors often treated her as an outsider. She kept at it. Later she moved to Washington and founded the Children's Defense Fund, which works for children in poverty. Her story reminds us that the movement needed lawyers as well as marchers, and that the courts, slow as they were, kept offering a path when other doors were closed.
📚 PART 4 · THE SCHOOLS

Freedom of Choice and Its Failure

Brown v. Board of Education was decided in 1954, but most Mississippi schools stayed segregated for fifteen more years. The state still ran a dual school system. After the Civil Rights Act of 1964, many districts adopted a freedom-of-choice plan. In theory, any family could pick any school. In practice, Black families who chose a white school faced threats, lost jobs, and sometimes had shots fired at their homes. Very few tried, and the schools stayed almost entirely separate.

In 1968 the Supreme Court ruled in Green v. New Kent County that such plans were not enough if the old system remained in place. Mississippi delayed. In 1969, lawyers for Black families in Holmes County asked the Court to end the delay. On October 29, 1969, in Alexander v. Holmes County Board of Education, the Court ruled that districts must stop operating segregated schools immediately. The phrase all deliberate speed had run out.

The Court's decision came after the federal government had asked for more time. The Court said no. It held that continued delay violated the rights of Black children, who had waited fifteen years since Brown. The ruling did not end the argument, but it ended the excuse of delay. For the first time, lawyers and parents could point to an order that left no room to wait.

Many Mississippi districts were ordered to merge their Black and white schools in the middle of the school year. Some white families responded by leaving the public schools. Beginning in the mid-1960s, they started private schools, often called segregation academies, and the number grew quickly after 1969. You will read more about the results in the next lesson.
