📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In January 1968, Robert Clark of Holmes County, the same county in the school case you just read about, walked into the Mississippi House of Representatives. He was its first Black member since the 1890s. What happened to a state when doors that had been shut for generations began to open?
Change came to Mississippi in the 1960s and after, but not all at once and not without cost. This lesson looks at the doors that opened, at the price some people paid, and at the questions that remain.

💡 GLOSSARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read.
historically Black college or university (his-TOR-ih-kul-ee blak KOL-ij or yoo-nih-VER-sih-tee) : A college or university founded to educate Black students at a time when most other colleges would not admit them.
racial reconciliation (RAY-shul rek-un-sil-ee-AY-shun) : The effort to repair relationships between groups after a history of injustice, by telling the truth and working together.
segregation academy (seg-rih-GAY-shun uh-KAD-uh-mee) : A private school started so that white children could avoid attending desegregated public schools.
📚 PART 1 · DOORS OPEN

A New Kind of Mississippi Government

Robert Clark was a teacher in Holmes County. He won his seat in the 1967 election, the first in which many Black Mississippians could vote in large numbers. For the next thirty-six years he served in the legislature, and in 1992 his colleagues chose him as speaker pro tempore. His career showed what was possible after the Voting Rights Act.

Others followed. Charles Evers, the brother of Medgar Evers, was elected mayor of Fayette in 1969. In 1986, Mike Espy, from the Delta, became the first Black Mississippian elected to Congress since Reconstruction. Over time, Mississippi came to have more Black elected officials than any other state. The state that had once worked hardest to keep Black citizens from the ballot now has a record that people across the country notice.

Change also came to ordinary places. After the Civil Rights Act of 1964, lunch counters, hotels, and theaters could no longer refuse to serve people because of their race. Many white business owners adjusted quietly. Some people were glad of the change, and some were not. The new rules were enforced, and over time they became part of daily life.

Local offices changed too. In the Delta and other counties where Black residents were a majority, they began winning seats on boards of supervisors, school boards, and city councils. You will see in Module 32 how those local boards work. These offices decide who gets roads, who gets jobs, and how tax money is spent, so the change mattered in everyday ways.

📚 PART 2 · THE COST OF CHANGE

Schools, Academies, and Jackson State

When the Supreme Court ordered the schools to merge in 1969, many districts did so in the winter of 1969 and 1970. Thousands of white families left the public schools. Many started or joined segregation academies, some of which were supported by the Citizens' Council. Public schools in many places became majority Black. They often had less money than before. The result was a state that was formally integrated, but where many children still went to school with classmates of the same race.

Violence returned in May 1970 at Jackson State College, a historically Black college or university in Jackson. Late on the night of May 14, police officers fired into a dormitory after a night of unrest and rumors of a sniper. Phillip Gibbs, a student, and James Green, a high school senior who was passing by, were killed. A dozen others were wounded. No officer was convicted. The shootings came only ten days after the deaths at Kent State in Ohio, but they received far less attention.

Why did Jackson State get less attention? Reporters were not there in the same numbers, and the victims were Black students at a Black college in Mississippi. The college later built a memorial to Gibbs and Green on campus, and its students and graduates have kept their story alive. The event is a reminder that some chapters of the nation's history reach the wider public only when someone decides to tell them.

Colleges were the next battleground. In 1975, a Delta farmer named Jake Ayers Sr. joined a lawsuit that charged the state with funding its white universities far more generously than its Black ones. The case went on for decades. In 1992 the Supreme Court ruled that Mississippi had not done enough to end the old system, and years later the state agreed to spend a large sum on its historically Black colleges.

The change cost something else, too. In many places, Black schools were closed or merged. Black principals, coaches, and teachers lost jobs, and school names, teams, and bands that communities had built over generations disappeared. Many Black families remember that loss alongside the gain. Change that opens one door can close another, and a fair account of this period must include both.

📚 PART 3 · FACING THE PAST

Late Justice and Honest Memory

Some of the old cases were reopened years later. Byron De La Beckwith was convicted in 1994, and Sam Bowers in 1998. Edgar Ray Killen was convicted in 2005. Each trial came long after the crime, and each came because someone refused to let the case rest. Families, reporters, prosecutors, and jurors all had a part.

The state's own institutions changed as well. In 1973 the legislature stopped funding the Sovereignty Commission that you met in Lesson 29.2, and its files were later opened for anyone to read. A government that once spied on citizens for asking for their rights had now made its record public. That is not a small thing. A community that lets its records be read has begun to tell the truth about itself.

Governor William Winter, who led the state from 1980 to 1984, spent much of his later life working on racial reconciliation. He helped found a center at the University of Mississippi, now called the Winter Institute, that brings people together to talk about the state's history. Reconciliation is not forgetting. It means telling the truth about what happened and working together to do better.

💡 MISSISSIPPI TODAY
A New Flag
For more than a century, Mississippi's state flag carried the Confederate battle emblem, which many Black Mississippians saw as a symbol of the system that had kept them from full citizenship. Over the years many leaders, businesses, and universities called for a change. In June 2020 the legislature voted to retire the old flag, and in November of that year voters approved a new design with a magnolia blossom. Many Mississippians welcomed the change. Others opposed it and wanted to keep the old flag. The change shows that what a state chooses to honor can be argued over, and changed, by its people.

So was Mississippi an open state? In many ways it was far more open than before. People could vote, run for office, eat in any restaurant, and attend any public school or university. Yet the state also kept some of the old gaps, in income, schooling, and trust. In the discussion, you will weigh which of these changes mattered most and why.

Open does not mean finished. Mississippi still faces gaps in income, in school funding, and in health. You will study some of those problems in Module 33. The people who worked for change in this module did not expect to solve everything. They expected to open doors and to let those who came after them walk through. Whether that work is carried on is a question for each new generation.
